\section{Probability theory}
For the measure-theoretic setting, a probability space is a measure
space $(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})$ with
$\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$. A real random variable is a measurable function
$X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. Its expectation is the integral
$\mathbb{E}X=\int_\Omega X\,d\mathbb{P}$ when $X$ is integrable;
no default value for an undefined signed integral is implicit.

In $L^1(\mathbb{P})$, random variables are identified when they agree
almost surely. The finite-measure conventions of the functional-analysis
section therefore apply directly. In particular, uniform integrability
controls more than boundedness of the expectations of absolute values.

Two uses of ``weak convergence'' should be distinguished. Weak convergence
in the Banach space $L^1(\mathbb{P})$ tests
$\mathbb{E}(ZX_n)$ against each fixed $Z\in L^\infty(\mathbb{P})$.
Convergence in distribution of real random variables instead tests
$\mathbb{E}(h(X_n))$ against bounded continuous functions
$h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$. The latter concerns their laws; it does
not even require the random variables to be defined on a common
probability space. A later theorem comparing these notions must state
its assumptions rather than identifying them by terminology.

\subsection{Two separations between modes of convergence}
The following examples make the distinction concrete; all variables
are defined on a single probability space.

\begin{example}[The same law need not give weak $L^1$ convergence]
Let $X$ take values $1$ and $-1$, each with probability $1/2$, and put
$X_n=(-1)^nX$. Every $X_n$ has the same law as $X$, so their laws
converge to the law of $X$. Nevertheless, testing weak $L^1$
convergence against the bounded random variable $Z=X$ gives
\[
\mathbb{E}(ZX_n)=(-1)^n,
\]
which does not converge. Thus the sequence has no weak $L^1$ limit.
Even uniform boundedness of the variables does not repair this
implication: information about joint position on the probability
space is absent from a law.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Weak $L^1$ convergence need not give convergence in probability]
On $[0,1)$ with Lebesgue probability measure, let
\[
r_n(t)=(-1)^{\lfloor2^nt\rfloor},\qquad n\geqslant1.
\]
The functions $r_n$ have norm one in $L^2$ and are pairwise orthogonal.
Indeed, for $m<n$, $r_m$ is constant on each dyadic interval of length
$2^{-m}$, whereas $r_n$ takes each sign on half of that interval.
For any real $g\in L^\infty$, put $c_n=\int_0^1gr_n$.
Expanding a square gives, for each $N$,
\[
0\leqslant\left\|g-\sum_{n=1}^Nc_nr_n\right\|_2^2
=\|g\|_2^2-\sum_{n=1}^Nc_n^2.
\]
Thus $c_n\to0$. The finite-measure identification of the $L^1$ dual
with $L^\infty$ shows $r_n\rightharpoonup0$ in $L^1$.
But $\mathbb{P}(|r_n|>1/2)=1$, so there is no convergence in
probability to zero. Their laws are all the symmetric two-point law,
not a sequence converging to the law of zero: the bounded continuous
test function $h(x)=\min(1,|x|)$ has expectation one for every $r_n$
and value zero at zero.
\end{example}

In contrast, convergence in probability together with uniform
integrability gives $L^1$ convergence by
Theorem~\ref{thm:vitali_finite}. It then gives convergence of
expectations because
$|\mathbb{E}X_n-\mathbb{E}X|\leqslant\mathbb{E}|X_n-X|$.
The concentration example $X_n=n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$ shows why
uniform integrability is needed: $X_n\to0$ in probability while
$\mathbb{E}X_n=1$ for all $n$.

\begin{corollary}[Weak convergence together with convergence in probability]
On a probability space, suppose $X_n\rightharpoonup X$ in $L^1$ and
$X_n\to Y$ in probability, where $Y$ is a measurable real random
variable. Then $Y=X$ almost surely and $\|X_n-X\|_1\to0$.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
Theorem~\ref{thm:weak_sequence_UI} gives uniform integrability of
$\{X_n:n\geq1\}$. Vitali's theorem gives $Y\in L^1$ and
$\|X_n-Y\|_1\to0$. For every bounded $Z$, both convergences then give
$\mathbb E(ZX)=\mathbb E(ZY)$. Taking $Z=\operatorname{sgn}(X-Y)$
yields $\mathbb E|X-Y|=0$, so $X=Y$ almost surely. Replacing $Y$ by
its equal $L^1$ class proves the norm-convergence statement.
\Done

\subsection{Conditional expectation from Hilbert representation}
Fix a finite positive measure space $(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$ and a
sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal A$ containing $\Omega$.
All functions in this subsection are real-valued. Write $L^p(\mathcal G)$
for the space formed using the restricted measure $\mu|_{\mathcal G}$.
Its natural map into $L^p(\mu)$ is isometric and injective: for two
$\mathcal G$-measurable representatives their difference and its
nonzero set are $\mathcal G$-measurable, and the restricted measure
has the same value on that set. We identify these spaces with their
images. An element of $L^p(\mathcal G)$ is required to have a
$\mathcal G$-measurable representative; arbitrary modifications on
null subsets are not asserted to preserve that measurability.

\begin{theorem}[Existence and uniqueness of conditional expectation]
\label{thm:conditional_expectation_finite}
For each $f\in L^1(\mu)$ there is a unique $g\in L^1(\mathcal G)$
such that
\[
\int_A g\,d\mu=\int_A f\,d\mu\qquad(A\in\mathcal G).
\]
Write $P_{\mathcal G}f=g$, also denoted
$\mathbb E_\mu[f\mid\mathcal G]$. This is a linear positive
projection onto $L^1(\mathcal G)$, preserves the total integral, and
is a contraction in $L^1$:
\[
\|P_{\mathcal G}f-P_{\mathcal G}k\|_1\leq\|f-k\|_1.
\]
The measure need not be complete, and its total mass need not be one.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
First establish uniqueness using only the displayed characterization.
If $g_1,g_2$ satisfy it, take $\mathcal G$-measurable representatives
and put $v=g_1-g_2$. For
$A_n=\{v\geq1/n\}\in\mathcal G$,
\[
0=\int_{A_n}v\,d\mu\geq\mu(A_n)/n,
\]
so each $A_n$ is null. Applying the same argument to $-v$ gives
$v=0$ almost everywhere. This proves uniqueness in the quotient.

For existence first suppose $f\in L^2(\mu)$. On the real Hilbert
space $L^2(\mathcal G)$ the functional
\[
u\longmapsto\int fu\,d\mu
\]
is continuous by Cauchy--Schwarz. Completeness of $L^2$ for the
restricted measure and Theorem~\ref{thm:real_Hilbert_representation}
give $g\in L^2(\mathcal G)$ with
$\int ug\,d\mu=\int uf\,d\mu$ for every $u\in L^2(\mathcal G)$.
Finite measure implies $g,f\in L^1$ and puts every
$\mathcal G$-indicator in $L^2(\mathcal G)$, giving the required
characterization. The sign of a $\mathcal G$-measurable representative
of $g$ is also in this Hilbert space, so
\[
\|g\|_1=\int\operatorname{sgn}(g)g\,d\mu
 =\int\operatorname{sgn}(g)f\,d\mu\leq\|f\|_1.
\]
Uniqueness makes this construction linear on $L^2$: linear combinations
of characterized outputs characterize the corresponding combinations
of inputs. Consequently it contracts $L^1$ distances between $L^2$
inputs.

For arbitrary $f\in L^1$, let
$f_n=\max(-n,\min(f,n))$. These are well-defined equivalence classes
in $L^2(\mu)$ and converge to $f$ in $L^1$, by integrable-tail
control and finite measure. The preceding contraction makes their
outputs $g_n$ Cauchy in $L^1(\mathcal G)$. Completeness for the
restricted measure supplies $g\in L^1(\mathcal G)$ with
$g_n\to g$ in that norm, hence also in ambient $L^1$. For every
$A\in\mathcal G$,
\[
\int_A g\,d\mu
 =\lim_n\int_A g_n\,d\mu
 =\lim_n\int_A f_n\,d\mu
 =\int_A f\,d\mu.
\]
The limits follow from the $L^1$ bound for integrals over a set.
This proves existence without invoking Radon--Nikodym.

Linearity now follows from uniqueness for all $L^1$ inputs. Taking
$A=\Omega$ proves preservation of the integral. If $f\geq0$ almost
everywhere, then $\int_Ag\,d\mu\geq0$ for every $A\in\mathcal G$.
The negative level sets $\{g\leq-1/n\}$ show, just as in the
uniqueness argument, that $g\geq0$ almost everywhere. Thus the map
is positive. If $f\in L^1(\mathcal G)$, it itself satisfies the
characterization, so $P_{\mathcal G}f=f$; this proves the projection
assertion. Finally the construction and $L^1$ convergence give
$\|P_{\mathcal G}f\|_1=\lim_n\|g_n\|_1\leq\lim_n\|f_n\|_1
=\|f\|_1$. Apply this to $f-k$ and use linearity for the distance
estimate. The zero-measure case is included: each of these quotient
spaces then consists of its zero class.
\Done

\begin{example}[Conditioning on a finite partition]
Let $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ be a finite measurable partition of $\Omega$,
and let $\mathcal G$ consist of its unions of atoms. For $f\in L^1(\mu)$,
\[
P_{\mathcal G}f
=\sum_{\substack{1\leq j\leq m\\\mu(A_j)>0}}
 \frac{\int_{A_j}f\,d\mu}{\mu(A_j)}\mathbf1_{A_j}
\quad\text{as an almost-everywhere class}.
\]
Indeed the displayed function is $\mathcal G$-measurable and integrable.
On a positive-measure atom its integral agrees with that of $f$ by
construction; on a null atom both integrals are zero. Finite additivity
therefore gives the defining identity on every union of atoms, and
uniqueness proves the formula. The displayed representative is zero on
null atoms. Any other finite constants on those atoms give the same
class and remain $\mathcal G$-measurable; there is no division by their
zero measures. Thus conditioning on this finite partition averages the
input over each observable atom without assigning a determined numerical
average on a null event.
\end{example}

For $f\in L^2$, the construction also gives the familiar orthogonality
identity $\int u(f-P_{\mathcal G}f)\,d\mu=0$ for
$u\in L^2(\mathcal G)$. Taking $u=P_{\mathcal G}f$ and expanding
the square gives
\[
\|f\|_2^2=\|P_{\mathcal G}f\|_2^2+
                 \|f-P_{\mathcal G}f\|_2^2.
\]
The extension to $L^1$ retains the integral characterization; it does
not assert that an arbitrary $L^1$ input has finite $L^2$ norm.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded tests, pullout, and the tower identities]
\label{prop:conditional_expectation_rules}
For $f\in L^1(\mu)$ and $u\in L^\infty(\mathcal G)$,
\[
\int uP_{\mathcal G}f\,d\mu=\int uf\,d\mu,
\qquad
P_{\mathcal G}(uf)=uP_{\mathcal G}f.
\]
If $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal A$ are
sub-sigma-algebras on the same carrier, then
\[
P_{\mathcal G}P_{\mathcal H}f=P_{\mathcal G}f,
\qquad P_{\mathcal H}P_{\mathcal G}f=P_{\mathcal G}f.
\]
\end{proposition}
\Proof
The first identity holds for $\mathcal G$-indicators by definition and
therefore for real $\mathcal G$-measurable simple functions by linearity.
A bounded representative of $u$ is uniformly approximated by such
simple functions. The approximation error in the two integrals is at
most $\|u-s\|_\infty(\|P_{\mathcal G}f\|_1+\|f\|_1)$,
which tends to zero. This proves the bounded-test identity.

The product $uP_{\mathcal G}f$ belongs to $L^1(\mathcal G)$.
Apply the first identity to $u\mathbf1_A$ for $A\in\mathcal G$:
its integral on $A$ equals $\int_A uf\,d\mu$. Uniqueness of
conditional expectation proves pullout.

For the first tower identity, take $A\in\mathcal G$. It also belongs
to $\mathcal H$, so successive uses of the characterization give
\[
\int_A P_{\mathcal G}P_{\mathcal H}f\,d\mu
 =\int_A P_{\mathcal H}f\,d\mu=\int_A f\,d\mu.
\]
The result is $\mathcal G$-measurable, hence equals
$P_{\mathcal G}f$ by uniqueness. For the second identity,
$P_{\mathcal G}f$ already has an $\mathcal H$-measurable
representative, so the projection property applies.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Uniform integrability under varying conditional expectations]
\label{cor:conditional_expectation_uniform_UI}
On the fixed finite positive measure space, let
$K\subseteq L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$ be uniformly integrable and let
$(\mathcal G_i)_{i\in I}$ be any set-indexed family of
sub-sigma-algebras of $\mathcal A$, each containing $\Omega$.
Then
\[
\{P_{\mathcal G_i}f:f\in K,\ i\in I\}
\]
is uniformly integrable. In particular this holds for a fixed
sub-sigma-algebra.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The conclusion is immediate if $K$ or $I$ is empty. Otherwise finite
measure and uniform integrability give $C=\sup_{f\in K}\|f\|_1<\infty$.
Positivity and linearity, applied to $-|f|\leq f\leq|f|$, give
\[
|P_{\mathcal G_i}f|\leq P_{\mathcal G_i}|f|
\quad\text{almost everywhere}.
\]
Choose a $\mathcal G_i$-measurable representative of the output, and
for $M>0$ put $A=\{|P_{\mathcal G_i}f|>M\}\in\mathcal G_i$.
The $L^1$ contraction and Markov's inequality give $\mu(A)\leq C/M$.
For any $R>0$, the defining integral identity for conditional
expectation, now applied to $|f|$, yields
\[
\begin{split}
\int_A|P_{\mathcal G_i}f|\,d\mu
&\leq\int_A P_{\mathcal G_i}|f|\,d\mu
 =\int_A|f|\,d\mu\\
&\leq\int_{\{|f|>R\}}|f|\,d\mu+R\mu(A)
 \leq\int_{\{|f|>R\}}|f|\,d\mu+\frac{RC}{M}.
\end{split}
\]
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $R$ making the first term less than
$\varepsilon/2$ uniformly for $f\in K$, and then choose $M$ making
$RC/M<\varepsilon/2$. These choices are independent of both $f$
and $i$, proving uniform integrability. This direct estimate uses
neither weak compactness nor a choice of representatives simultaneously
for the whole indexed family.
\Done

\subsection{Probability densities and compactness}
Fix a finite positive measure space $(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$.
A probability density relative to $\mu$ is an $f\in L^1(\mu)$ with
$f\geq0$ almost everywhere and $\int f\,d\mu=1$. It determines the
probability measure $\nu_f(A)=\int_A f\,d\mu$. If $\mu(\Omega)=0$,
there are no such densities; the statements about a sequence of them
then have no instances.

\begin{proposition}[The density set is weakly closed]
\label{prop:density_set_weak_closed}
The set
\[
\mathcal D_\mu=\{f\in L^1(\mu;\mathbb R):f\geq0\text{ a.e.},\ \int f\,d\mu=1\}
\]
is weakly closed in $L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
The indicator of every measurable set $A$ is a bounded test function,
so $f\mapsto\int_A f\,d\mu$ is weakly continuous. The same holds
for the total integral, using the constant test function one. Therefore
\[
\left\{f:\int f\,d\mu=1\right\}
 \cap\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal A}\left\{f:\int_A f\,d\mu\geq0\right\}
\]
is weakly closed. We claim it equals $\mathcal D_\mu$. Every density
belongs to it. Conversely, for a member $f$ choose a measurable
representative and put $A_k=\{f\leq-1/k\}$. The asserted integral
inequalities give
\[
0\leq\int_{A_k}f\,d\mu\leq-\mu(A_k)/k,
\]
so $\mu(A_k)=0$. Their countable union is $\{f<0\}$, proving
$f\geq0$ almost everywhere. Its total integral is already one,
which proves the claimed equality and weak closedness.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Weak limits and subsequences of densities]
Every weak $L^1$ limit of a net of probability densities is a
probability density. In particular every uniformly integrable sequence
of probability densities has a subsequence converging weakly in $L^1$
to a probability density.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The weakly closed density set contains every limit of a net taking
values in it. For a uniformly integrable sequence,
Theorem~\ref{thm:DP_sequential_finite} supplies a weakly convergent
subsequence; its limit belongs to $\mathcal D_\mu$ by closedness.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Weak compactness of families of densities]
For $K\subseteq\mathcal D_\mu$, uniform integrability is equivalent
to compactness of its weak closure in $L^1$. When these conditions
hold, every member of that compact closure is still a probability
density. In particular, a weakly closed uniformly integrable family
of probability densities is weakly compact.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The equivalence is Theorem~\ref{thm:DP_weak_compact_finite}. Since
$\mathcal D_\mu$ is weakly closed and contains $K$, it contains the
weak closure of $K$. If $K$ itself is weakly closed, that closure
is $K$. The empty family, including the only possible family when
$\mu(\Omega)=0$, satisfies the conclusions directly.
\Done

\begin{lemma}[Integrable observables on a dominated interval]
\label{lem:dominated_observable_continuity}
Let $h\in L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$ be nonnegative almost everywhere and put
\[
D_h=\{f\in L^1(\mu;\mathbb R):0\leq f\leq h\text{ a.e.}\}.
\]
If $\psi$ is a measurable real function with
$\int|\psi|h\,d\mu<\infty$, then
\[
\Phi_\psi:D_h\longrightarrow\mathbb R,\qquad
\Phi_\psi(f)=\int\psi f\,d\mu
\]
is continuous for the relative weak topology of $L^1$.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
Choose a nonnegative measurable representative of $h$. The estimate
$|\psi f|\leq|\psi|h$ almost everywhere makes every displayed integral
finite and independent of the representative of $f$. Put
$\psi_m=\max(-m,\min(\psi,m))$. Each bounded test
$\Phi_{\psi_m}$ is weakly continuous on all of $L^1$, and
\[
\sup_{f\in D_h}|\Phi_\psi(f)-\Phi_{\psi_m}(f)|
\leq\int_{\{|\psi|>m\}}|\psi|h\,d\mu\longrightarrow0.
\]
Dominated convergence proves the limit, since $\psi$ is finite-valued
and $|\psi|h$ is integrable. Thus the restrictions of the bounded-test
functionals converge uniformly to $\Phi_\psi$ on $D_h$. Explicitly,
given a tolerance, choose $m$ so that both approximation errors are
less than one third of it, and then a relative weak neighbourhood
controlling the difference of the two $\Phi_{\psi_m}$ values by its
remaining third. This proves continuity, for nets as well as sequences.
\Done

The conclusion is restricted to the dominated interval. An unbounded
observable need not define a finite continuous functional on all of
$L^1$; domination supplies the uniform approximation used in the proof.

\begin{theorem}[Attainment under domination and moment constraints]
\label{thm:attained_density_expectations}
Let $h\in L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$ be nonnegative almost everywhere.
Let $(\phi_i)_{i\in I}$ be a set-indexed family of measurable real
functions with $\int|\phi_i|h\,d\mu<\infty$ for every $i$, and let
$(c_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of real numbers. Suppose the family
\[
C=\left\{f\in\mathcal D_\mu:
 f\leq h\text{ a.e.},\quad
 \int\phi_i f\,d\mu=c_i\text{ for every }i\in I\right\}
\]
is nonempty. Then $C$ is weakly compact. For every measurable real
observable $\psi$ with $\int|\psi|h\,d\mu<\infty$, there are
$f_-,f_+\in C$ such that
\[
\int\psi f_-\,d\mu
 \leq\int\psi f\,d\mu
 \leq\int\psi f_+\,d\mu\qquad(f\in C).
\]
No countability or finiteness condition on $I$ is required.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
The condition $f\leq h$ almost everywhere is equivalent to
\[
\int_A f\,d\mu\leq\int_A h\,d\mu\qquad(A\in\mathcal A).
\]
One implication is monotonicity of the integral. For the converse,
apply the positivity argument from Proposition~\ref{prop:density_set_weak_closed}
to $h-f$: its integral on every measurable set is nonnegative, so
$h-f\geq0$ almost everywhere. Each displayed inequality defines a
weakly closed half-space. Together with the weakly closed density set,
these inequalities make
$C_0=\{f\in\mathcal D_\mu:f\leq h\text{ a.e.}\}$ weakly closed.

All members of $C_0$ satisfy $0\leq f\leq h$ almost everywhere. Consequently
\[
\sup_{f\in C_0}\int_{\{f>M\}} f\,d\mu
 \leq\int_{\{h>M\}}h\,d\mu\longrightarrow0.
\]
The vanishing-tail lemma makes $C_0$ uniformly integrable. Theorem~
\ref{thm:DP_weak_compact_finite} makes $C_0$ weakly compact.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:dominated_observable_continuity}, each moment map
is continuous on $C_0\subseteq D_h$. Therefore $C$ is an intersection
of relatively closed subsets of $C_0$, and is compact, even for
arbitrarily many set-indexed constraints. The same lemma makes the
objective continuous on $C$, so its image is a nonempty compact subset of
$\mathbb R$. Such a subset has a minimum and maximum: it is bounded,
and its infimum and supremum belong to it by closedness. Their preimages
in $C$ give $f_-$ and $f_+$.
\Done

Bounded observables and bounded moment functions satisfy these weighted
integrability hypotheses automatically, since $h\in L^1$. Thus the
theorem includes the bounded-test case while also permitting unbounded
observables whose absolute values are integrable against the common
dominating density bound.

The nonemptiness hypothesis is a genuine feasibility condition on the
moments. Without moment constraints, the dominated density family is
nonempty exactly when $\int h\,d\mu\geq1$. Necessity follows by
integrating $f\leq h$. For sufficiency put
$H=\int h\,d\mu\geq1$ and take $f=h/H$: this is a density and
$0\leq f\leq h$. Extra prescribed moments may make the family empty,
so the theorem does not assert feasibility of arbitrary numerical data.

\begin{example}[Attaining expectations of an unbounded observable]
On $(0,1)$ with Lebesgue measure, take $h=2$ and
$\psi(x)=x^{-1/2}$. Although $\psi$ is unbounded,
$\int_0^1|\psi|h\,dx=4<\infty$. Among all probability densities
with $0\leq f\leq2$ almost everywhere, the maximum and minimum
expectations are attained by
\[
f_+=2\mathbf1_{(0,1/2)},\qquad
f_-=2\mathbf1_{(1/2,1)},
\]
respectively, and their values are
\[
\int_0^1\psi f_+\,dx=2\sqrt2,\qquad
\int_0^1\psi f_-\,dx=4-2\sqrt2.
\]
Both functions are feasible because their integrals are one.
To verify optimality without assuming it from the shape of $\psi$,
subtract the threshold value $\psi(1/2)=\sqrt2$. Since all feasible
densities have the same mass,
\[
\int\psi(f-f_+)\,dx=\int(\psi-\sqrt2)(f-f_+)\,dx\leq0.
\]
On $(0,1/2)$ the first factor is positive and the second is nonpositive;
on $(1/2,1)$ their signs reverse. Both products are integrable by the
common bound $h$. Replacing $f_+$ by $f_-$ makes the product nonnegative
on both intervals, proving the minimum assertion. Finally,
$\int_0^b x^{-1/2}\,dx=2\sqrt b$ gives the two displayed values.
Thus domination permits optimization of this unbounded test even though
it is not an element of the ambient $L^\infty$ dual-test space.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Setwise testing for densities]
For probability densities $f_n$ and $f$, the following are equivalent:
\[
f_n\rightharpoonup f\text{ in }L^1(\mu),
\qquad
\nu_{f_n}(A)\longrightarrow\nu_f(A)
\text{ for every }A\in\mathcal A.
\]
\end{proposition}
\Proof
Weak convergence implies the displayed convergence by testing indicators.
Conversely, the assumption gives convergence of integrals against every
real measurable simple function. For $g\in L^\infty$, choose a bounded
representative and a simple $s$ with $\|g-s\|_\infty<\varepsilon$.
Then
\[
\left|\int g(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|
\leq\left|\int s(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|
 +\varepsilon(\|f_n\|_1+\|f\|_1)
=\left|\int s(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|+2\varepsilon.
\]
Take the upper limit in $n$, then let $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero.
The dual identification in Theorem~\ref{thm:L1_dual_finite} gives weak
$L^1$ convergence. The uniform norm bound, here exactly one, is what
allows indicators to be extended to all bounded tests.
\Done

\subsection{Convergence tested on a generating algebra}
Continue with the finite positive measure space
$(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$. An \emph{algebra of sets} on $\Omega$
contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements relative to
$\Omega$ and finite unions. It is therefore a $\pi$-system as well.
Let $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A$ be such an algebra with
$\sigma(\mathcal B)=\mathcal A$. No countability assumption on
$\mathcal B$ is imposed.

\begin{lemma}[Approximation by a generating algebra]
\label{lem:algebra_measure_approximation}
For every $A\in\mathcal A$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there is
$B\in\mathcal B$ with $\mu(A\mathbin\triangle B)<\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
Let $\mathcal D$ consist of the measurable sets with this approximation
property for every positive tolerance. It contains $\mathcal B$ and
hence $\Omega$. It is closed under complements because
\[
(\Omega\setminus A)\mathbin\triangle(\Omega\setminus B)
=A\mathbin\triangle B,
\]
and complements of members of $\mathcal B$ remain in $\mathcal B$.

Suppose $A_n\in\mathcal D$ are pairwise disjoint and put
$A=\bigcup_nA_n$. Since $\mu$ is finite, countable additivity gives
$\sum_n\mu(A_n)=\mu(A)<\infty$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose
$N\geq1$ with $\mu(\bigcup_{n>N}A_n)<\varepsilon/2$.
For $1\leq n\leq N$, choose $B_n\in\mathcal B$ with
$\mu(A_n\mathbin\triangle B_n)<\varepsilon/(2N)$.
The set $B=\bigcup_{n=1}^N B_n$ is in $\mathcal B$, and
\[
A\mathbin\triangle B
\subseteq\bigcup_{n>N}A_n\ \cup
          \bigcup_{n=1}^N(A_n\mathbin\triangle B_n).
\]
Subadditivity gives $\mu(A\mathbin\triangle B)<\varepsilon$.
Thus $\mathcal D$ is a Dynkin system containing the $\pi$-system
$\mathcal B$. Theorem~\ref{thm:pi_lambda} gives
$\mathcal A=\sigma(\mathcal B)\subseteq\mathcal D$, proving the
claim. Only finitely many approximating sets were chosen for each
fixed tolerance; no enumeration of $\mathcal B$ was needed.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[A generating-algebra test for weak $L^1$ convergence]
\label{thm:generating_algebra_weak_L1}
Let $f_n,f\in L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$. Then $f_n$ converges weakly
to $f$ if and only if $\{f_n:n\geq1\}$ is uniformly integrable and
\[
\int_B f_n\,d\mu\longrightarrow\int_B f\,d\mu
\qquad\text{for every }B\in\mathcal B.
\]
The functions need not be positive or normalized densities.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Weak convergence gives the displayed limits by testing indicators,
and gives uniform integrability by
Theorem~\ref{thm:weak_sequence_UI}.

For the converse, adjoin $f$ to the uniformly integrable family.
Its vanishing tail integrals show that the enlarged family is still
uniformly integrable. The uniform-small-set lemma therefore gives,
for every $\eta>0$, a $\delta>0$ such that
\[
\mu(C)<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\quad
\int_C|f_n|\,d\mu<\eta/2\text{ for all }n,
\quad \int_C|f|\,d\mu<\eta/2.
\]
For an arbitrary $A\in\mathcal A$, choose $B\in\mathcal B$ with
$\mu(A\mathbin\triangle B)<\delta$ using the lemma. Then
\[
\left|\int_A(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|
\leq\left|\int_B(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|
  +\int_{A\mathbin\triangle B}(|f_n|+|f|)\,d\mu.
\]
The first term tends to zero by hypothesis and the second is less
than $\eta$, uniformly in $n$. Since $\eta$ is arbitrary,
$\int_A(f_n-f)\to0$ for every measurable $A$. Taking finite linear
combinations gives convergence against every real measurable simple
function.

Uniform integrability on the finite measure space also gives
$C=\sup_n\|f_n\|_1+\|f\|_1<\infty$.
For $g\in L^\infty$, choose a bounded measurable representative.
For each $a>0$, rounding its values to a sufficiently fine finite
grid gives a measurable simple $s$ with $\|g-s\|_\infty<a$.
Consequently
\[
\left|\int g(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|
\leq\left|\int s(f_n-f)\,d\mu\right|+aC.
\]
Take the upper limit in $n$, then let $a$ decrease to zero. Thus all
bounded-test integrals converge, and the dual identification in
Theorem~\ref{thm:L1_dual_finite} proves weak $L^1$ convergence.
\Done

For densities, this criterion permits setwise convergence to be checked
on a generating algebra once uniform integrability is established.
The four-point example in the measure chapter only had agreement on
an arbitrary generating family. Its generated algebra also contains
$\{1\}$, which distinguishes the two measures. Here closure under
the algebra operations supplies the needed testing sets, while uniform
integrability makes approximation in $\mu$-measure control the integrals
uniformly throughout the sequence.

\begin{example}[Algebra tests and bounded norms do not replace uniform integrability]
Let $\Omega=[0,1]$ with its Borel sigma-algebra and Lebesgue measure
restricted to that sigma-algebra. Let $\mathcal B$ be the algebra
formed by finite Boolean combinations, relative to $\Omega$, of the
sets $\Omega\cap(p,q)$ with rational $p<q$. It generates the Borel
sigma-algebra: these sets form a countable base for the relative usual
topology, so every relatively open set is a countable union of them;
conversely all the generators are Borel. We use the Borel space here,
without asserting that this algebra generates the completed Lebesgue
sigma-algebra.

Fix an irrational $a\in(0,1)$ and put
\[
\delta_n=\frac{\min(a,1-a)}{n+1},\qquad
f_n=\delta_n^{-1}
\bigl(\mathbf1_{(a,a+\delta_n)}-\mathbf1_{(a-\delta_n,a)}\bigr).
\]
Both intervals lie in $[0,1]$, are disjoint, and have length
$\delta_n$. Hence $\int f_n=0$ and $\|f_n\|_1=2$.

Each fixed $B\in\mathcal B$ is constant in membership on some open
interval about $a$. Indeed, its finite Boolean expression involves
only finitely many rational endpoints, none equal to $a$; choose a
small interval avoiding all these endpoints. Every generator in that
expression, and therefore $B$ itself, either contains this entire
interval or misses it. For all sufficiently large $n$, the support
of $f_n$ lies in that interval. Thus
\[
\int_B f_n\,d\mu=0\quad\text{eventually for every fixed }B\in\mathcal B.
\]
These tests converge to the corresponding integrals of $f=0$.
Nevertheless the bounded Borel test $g=\mathbf1_{(a,1]}$ satisfies
$\int gf_n\,d\mu=1$ for every $n$, so $f_n$ does not converge
weakly to zero. The family also fails uniform integrability directly:
for any $M>0$, choose $n$ with $\delta_n^{-1}>M$. Then
\[
\int_{\{|f_n|>M\}}|f_n|\,d\mu=2.
\]
The bounded norms and convergence on the entire generating algebra
therefore do not suffice without the uniform-integrability condition.
\end{example}

\subsection{Total variation of probability densities}
\begin{theorem}[Total variation and Scheffe's lemma]
\label{thm:scheffe_density}
For probability densities $f,g$, define the total variation distance by
\[
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_f,\nu_g)
 =\sup_{A\in\mathcal A}|\nu_f(A)-\nu_g(A)|.
\]
Then $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_f,\nu_g)=\tfrac12\|f-g\|_1$.
If probability densities $f_n$ converge almost everywhere to a probability
density $f$, then $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$, hence their measures converge in
this distance. The assumption that the limit has integral one is essential.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Choose nonnegative measurable representatives of the densities; changing
them on their measurable null sets does not change any conclusion.
Put $h=f-g$ and $h^+=\max(h,0)$, $h^-=\max(-h,0)$.
Since $\int h=0$, their integrals agree and each is
$\tfrac12\int|h|$. For every measurable $A$,
\[
-\int h^-\leq\int_A h\leq\int h^+.
\]
The upper bound is attained at $A=\{h\geq0\}$, proving the distance
identity. Next, $\min(f_n,f)\to f$ almost everywhere and
$0\leq\min(f_n,f)\leq f$. Dominated convergence gives
$\int\min(f_n,f)\to1$. The elementary identity
$|f_n-f|=f_n+f-2\min(f_n,f)$ therefore yields
\[
\|f_n-f\|_1=2-2\int\min(f_n,f)\,d\mu\longrightarrow0.
\]
The concentration densities $n\mathbf1_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$ converge
almost everywhere to zero, whose integral is zero, and their norms stay
one. Thus the conclusion fails if the normalization of the limit is dropped.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Convergence in measure of normalized densities]
For probability densities $f_n,f$ relative to the fixed finite measure,
the following are equivalent:
\[
f_n\to f\text{ in measure},\qquad
\|f_n-f\|_1\to0,\qquad
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{f_n},\nu_f)\to0.
\]
In particular, convergence in measure to a normalized density supplies
uniform integrability of the sequence; it need not be assumed separately.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
Norm convergence implies convergence in measure by
\[
\mu(\{|f_n-f|>a\})\leq a^{-1}\|f_n-f\|_1\qquad(a>0),
\]
and the total variation identity proves the last equivalence.
Suppose now that convergence in measure holds but norm convergence does
not. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ and a subsequence with
$\|f_{n_k}-f\|_1\geq\varepsilon$ for all $k$. The almost-everywhere
subsequence lemma supplies a further subsequence converging to $f$
almost everywhere. Scheffe's lemma, Theorem~\ref{thm:scheffe_density},
makes its $L^1$ distances tend to zero, a contradiction.
Finally, the converse Vitali implication proved in the functional-analysis
section says that an $L^1$-convergent sequence is uniformly integrable.
Both positivity and preservation of total mass enter through Scheffe's
lemma; convergence in measure of arbitrary integrable functions alone
does not give this conclusion.
\Done

\begin{example}[Setwise convergence need not be total variation convergence]
For the Rademacher functions above, $f_n=1+r_n$ is a probability density
on $[0,1)$: it takes values zero and two and has integral one.
The proved weak convergence $r_n\rightharpoonup0$ gives
$f_n\rightharpoonup1$, hence setwise convergence of the associated
measures. But $\|f_n-1\|_1=\|r_n\|_1=1$, so their total variation
distance from Lebesgue probability measure is always $1/2$.
Even uniform boundedness of the densities does not turn setwise
convergence into total variation convergence. It does supply uniform
integrability and hence compactness of the weak closure, as well as the
weak subsequence conclusion above. Neither conclusion is norm compactness.
\end{example}
