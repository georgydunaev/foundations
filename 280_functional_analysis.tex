\section{Functional analysis}
\label{sec:functional_analysis}
This section specifies a setting for the intended applications to
Dunford--Pettis compactness. The spaces here have set-sized carriers and
use classical real and complex analysis. The earlier class-theoretic framework can
express their constructions, but proper-class-valued families and
partial descriptors do not alter the analytic definitions by default.
For Hahn--Banach and the subsequent analytic development, we work in
classical set theory with Choice. Zorn's lemma is used in the extension
argument; the nested constructions in Baire's theorem and the open
mapping theorem use dependent choice. Some individual results need
less, as their proofs indicate. This convention does not strengthen
the assumptions of the separate countable-choice metric theorem.

The companion manuscript on the Dunford--Pettis theorem is not included
in this checkout. The development below proves the finite-measure criterion
in both sequential and topological forms, together with its analytic
prerequisites. It does not
reconstruct that missing manuscript or assert a formalization in Obelisk.

\subsection{Linear structure and topology}
Fix the scalar field $\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. A
\emph{topological vector space} is a vector space $E$ equipped with a
topology for which addition $E\times E\to E$ and scalar multiplication
$\mathbb{K}\times E\to E$ are continuous, using the product topologies.
When Hausdorffness is needed, it is an explicit assumption. A locally
convex topology has a base of convex neighbourhoods of zero.

A norm $\|\cdot\|$ gives the metric $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$ and hence a
Hausdorff vector-space topology. A \emph{Banach space} is a normed
vector space in which every Cauchy sequence converges in this metric.
Completeness is a condition on the specified norm topology; it is not
implied by the existence of an algebraic vector-space structure.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded linear maps and continuity]
For a linear map $T:E\to F$ between normed spaces, continuity is
equivalent to the existence of $C\geqslant0$ such that
\[
\|Tx\|_F\leqslant C\|x\|_E\qquad(x\in E).
\]
\end{lemma}
\Proof
The inequality gives
$\|Tx-Ty\|_F\leqslant C\|x-y\|_E$, hence continuity.
Conversely, continuity at zero supplies $\delta>0$ such that
$\|u\|_E<\delta$ implies $\|Tu\|_F<1$. For $x\ne0$, apply this to
$u=\delta x/(2\|x\|_E)$ and use linearity to obtain
$\|Tx\|_F\leqslant(2/\delta)\|x\|_E$. The inequality at zero follows
from $T0=0$.
\Done
An operator defined only on a subspace $D(T)\subseteq E$ has that
subspace as its domain. Calling such an operator unbounded does not
supply values at points outside $D(T)$.

\subsection{Weak topology and the continuous dual}
Let $E'$ be the space of continuous linear maps $\ell:E\to\mathbb{K}$.
The weak topology $\sigma(E,E')$ is the coarsest topology on $E$
making every $\ell\in E'$ continuous. A basic neighbourhood of $x$
is determined by finitely many functionals and positive tolerances:
\[
\{y\in E\mid |\ell_j(y-x)|<\varepsilon_j,
  \ j=1,\ldots,m\}.
\]
Thus a net $x_i$ converges weakly to $x$ exactly when
$\ell(x_i)\to\ell(x)$ for every $\ell\in E'$. The same criterion
applies to sequences. For a normed space $E$, norm convergence implies weak convergence,
because $|\ell(x_i-x)|\leqslant\|\ell\|\,\|x_i-x\|$.

Weak convergence uses all continuous linear functionals on $E$.
On a dual space $E'$, the weak-star topology $\sigma(E',E)$ instead
uses evaluations $\ell\mapsto\ell(x)$ at vectors $x\in E$.
These are distinct definitions; a compactness statement must identify
which topology it uses.

\subsection{Dual completeness and what weak convergence detects}
The operator norm on $E'$ is
\[
\|\ell\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leqslant1}|\ell(x)|.
\]
The bounded-map lemma makes this finite and gives
$|\ell(x)|\leqslant\|\ell\|\,\|x\|$. It is a norm: in particular,
vanishing on the unit ball implies vanishing everywhere by scaling.

\begin{theorem}[The continuous dual is complete]
For every normed space $E$, its continuous dual $E'$ is a Banach space,
even if $E$ itself is not complete.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Let $(\ell_n)$ be Cauchy in operator norm. For each $x\in E$,
\[
|\ell_n(x)-\ell_m(x)|\leqslant\|\ell_n-\ell_m\|\,\|x\|,
\]
so completeness of the scalar field gives a limit $\ell(x)$.
Passing to limits in addition and scalar multiplication shows that
$\ell$ is linear. A Cauchy sequence is norm bounded, say
$\|\ell_n\|\leqslant C$, and passage to the limit gives
$|\ell(x)|\leqslant C\|x\|$. Thus $\ell\in E'$.
For any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ so that
$\|\ell_n-\ell_m\|<\varepsilon$ for $n,m\geqslant N$.
Letting $m$ tend to infinity gives
$|\ell_n(x)-\ell(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon\|x\|$ for every $x$ and
$n\geqslant N$. Taking the supremum on the unit ball proves
$\|\ell_n-\ell\|\leqslant\varepsilon$, hence norm convergence.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Norm-preserving Hahn--Banach extension]
Assume Zorn's lemma in the surrounding set theory. A bounded linear
functional $f$ on a linear subspace $D$ of a real or complex normed
space $E$ extends to a bounded linear functional on $E$ with the same
norm. The subspace need not be closed.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
First work over the reals. We prove the more general extension statement
for a sublinear function $p:E\to\mathbb R$: assume $p(x+y)\leq p(x)+p(y)$,
$p(tx)=tp(x)$ for $t\geq0$, and $f(x)\leq p(x)$ on $D$.
If $a\notin D$, extend to $D+\mathbb Ra$ by
$g(x+ta)=f(x)+tc$. Domination by $p$ holds exactly when
\[
\sup_{y\in D}\{f(y)-p(y-a)\}
\leq c\leq
\inf_{x\in D}\{p(x+a)-f(x)\}.
\]
The left quantities do not exceed the right quantities: for $x,y\in D$,
\[
f(x)+f(y)=f(x+y)\leq p(x+y)\leq p(x+a)+p(y-a).
\]
Both indexing sets are nonempty, and fixing one element on either side
bounds the other. Completeness of $\mathbb R$ therefore supplies such a
$c$. For $t>0$, apply the upper inequality to $x/t$ and multiply by $t$;
for $t<0$, apply the lower inequality to $x/(-t)$ and multiply by $-t$.
These give $g(x+ta)\leq p(x+ta)$; the case $t=0$ is the original bound.

Order all dominated linear extensions of $f$ by extension of their
graphs. This is a set, since each graph is a subset of $E\times\mathbb R$.
A nonempty chain has an upper bound obtained by union: its domains form
an increasing family of subspaces, and compatibility makes the union a
linear dominated extension. For the empty chain, the original $f$ is
an upper bound. Zorn's lemma gives a maximal extension.
The one-dimensional construction shows its domain is all of $E$.
Taking $p(x)=\|f\|\,\|x\|$ and applying domination to $x$ and $-x$
gives $|g(x)|\leq\|f\|\,\|x\|$. Restriction to $D$ gives the reverse
norm inequality, so the norm is preserved (also when $D=\{0\}$).

For a complex space, extend the real-linear functional
$u=\operatorname{Re}f$ from the underlying real subspace of $D$ to a
real-linear $g:E\to\mathbb R$ with bound $\|f\|$ by the real result.
Set $F(x)=g(x)-i g(ix)$. Real linearity and
$F(ix)=iF(x)$ imply complex linearity. On $D$, the identity
$\operatorname{Re}f(ix)=-\operatorname{Im}f(x)$ gives $F=f$.
For $F(x)\ne0$, choose a scalar $\lambda$ with $|\lambda|=1$ and
$\lambda F(x)=|F(x)|$. Then
\[
|F(x)|=\operatorname{Re}F(\lambda x)=g(\lambda x)
\leq\|f\|\,\|\lambda x\|=\|f\|\,\|x\|.
\]
The same bound is immediate if $F(x)=0$. Restriction again proves
$\|F\|=\|f\|$.
\Done

The choice principle enters at Zorn's lemma, not at the elementary
one-dimensional extension. The following consequences use this theorem
in the classical analytic setting; they are not asserted in every
choice-free foundational setting explored earlier.

\begin{proposition}[Norming functionals and weak separation]
For every $x\in E$,
\[
\|x\|=\sup_{\ell\in E',\ \|\ell\|\leqslant1}|\ell(x)|.
\]
Consequently $\sigma(E,E')$ is Hausdorff, and a weak limit is unique.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
The inequality from right to left follows from the operator-norm bound.
For $x\ne0$, define on the one-dimensional subspace spanned by $x$
the functional $a x\mapsto a\|x\|$. Its norm is one over either
scalar field. Hahn--Banach extends it to $\ell\in E'$ of norm one,
with $\ell(x)=\|x\|$. At $x=0$ both sides vanish.
If $x\ne y$, a norming functional for $x-y$ has
$\ell(x)\ne\ell(y)$. Disjoint scalar neighbourhoods of those values
have disjoint weakly open inverse images containing $x$ and $y$.
This is Hausdorffness; uniqueness of limits follows by intersecting
the corresponding eventual neighbourhood conditions.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Weak lower semicontinuity of the norm]
Every norm-closed ball is weakly closed. If a sequence $x_n$ converges
weakly to $x$, then
\[
\|x\|\leqslant\liminf_n\|x_n\|.
\]
\end{corollary}
\Proof
For $r\geqslant0$, the norming formula gives
\[
\{y:\|y-x\|\leqslant r\}
=\bigcap_{\ell\in E',\ \|\ell\|\leqslant1}
 \{y:|\ell(y-x)|\leqslant r\}.
\]
Every set on the right is weakly closed, by continuity of its defining
functional. For the sequential assertion, if $x\ne0$, choose a norming
functional $\ell$ for $x$. Then
$|\ell(x_n)|\to\|x\|$ and $|\ell(x_n)|\leqslant\|x_n\|$.
Taking lower limits proves the inequality. The case $x=0$ is immediate.
\Done

\begin{proposition}[Weak neighbourhoods in infinite dimensions]
If $E$ is infinite-dimensional, every weak neighbourhood of zero
contains an unbounded linear subspace. Hence the weak and norm
topologies on $E$ are different.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
Such a neighbourhood contains one defined by finitely many functionals
$\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_m$ and positive tolerances. It contains their common
kernel. Choose $m+1$ linearly independent vectors in $E$. Their images
under $x\mapsto(\ell_1(x),\ldots,\ell_m(x))\in\mathbb K^m$ are linearly
dependent, so a nonzero linear combination of the chosen vectors lies
in the common kernel. All scalar multiples of this nonzero vector
also lie there, and their norms are unbounded. The norm unit ball
therefore cannot be a weak neighbourhood of zero, although it is a
norm neighbourhood. Norm convergence still implies weak convergence;
the topologies need not coincide.
\Done

This final argument uses only finite-dimensional linear dependence,
not Hahn--Banach. It also explains why finitely many tests cannot
control norm size in an infinite-dimensional space, even though the
whole dual determines the norm by the preceding proposition.

\subsection{Completeness and uniform boundedness}
\begin{lemma}[Baire's theorem for complete metric spaces]
In a nonempty complete metric space, the intersection of countably many
dense open sets is dense. Equivalently, such a space cannot be the union
of countably many closed sets all having empty interior.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
Let $G_n$ be dense and open, and let $U$ be any nonempty open set.
Choose a closed ball $B_1$ of positive radius at most $2^{-1}$ contained
in $U\cap G_1$. Inductively choose a closed ball $B_{n+1}$ of positive
radius at most $2^{-(n+1)}$ contained in the intersection of $G_{n+1}$
with the open ball having the same centre and radius as $B_n$.
Density and openness make each choice possible; balls with slightly
smaller radii ensure closed containment. Their centres form a Cauchy
sequence because the balls are nested and their diameters tend to zero.
Completeness gives a limit $x$. Each $B_n$ is closed and contains all
later centres, so $x\in B_n$ for every $n$. Thus
$x\in U\cap\bigcap_nG_n$. Taking complements proves the equivalent
closed-set statement. The successive choices are made in the classical
set-theoretic setting of this chapter.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Uniform boundedness principle]
Let $E$ be Banach, $F$ a normed space, and $(T_i)_{i\in I}$ a set-indexed
family of bounded linear maps $E\to F$. If
$\sup_{i\in I}\|T_i x\|<\infty$ for every $x\in E$, then
$\sup_{i\in I}\|T_i\|<\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
The empty family is immediate. Put
\[
A_n=\{x\in E:\|T_i x\|\leq n\text{ for every }i\in I\},
\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
\]
Continuity makes each $A_n$ closed, and pointwise boundedness gives
$E=\bigcup_nA_n$. Baire's theorem gives an $A_n$ containing an open
ball of radius $r>0$ about some $x_0$. For $\|h\|<r$, both $x_0+h$
and $x_0$ belong to $A_n$, hence
$\|T_i h\|\leq\|T_i(x_0+h)\|+\|T_i x_0\|\leq2n$ for every $i$.
For any $u$ with $\|u\|\leq1$, take $h=(r/2)u$ to obtain
$\|T_i u\|\leq4n/r$, uniformly in $i$. Taking suprema proves the claim.
Completeness of $F$ is not used.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Weakly convergent sequences are norm bounded]
If a sequence $(x_n)$ in a normed space $E$ converges weakly, then
$\sup_n\|x_n\|<\infty$. The space $E$ need not be complete.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
On the Banach space $E'$, consider the bounded linear maps
$J_n:E'\to\mathbb K$, $J_n(\ell)=\ell(x_n)$.
For each $\ell$, weak convergence makes $(J_n(\ell))$ a convergent,
hence bounded, scalar sequence. Uniform boundedness gives
$\sup_n\|J_n\|<\infty$. The norming-functional formula gives
$\|J_n\|=\|x_n\|$, proving the assertion.
\Done

The sequential hypothesis is significant. A convergent net need not
have bounded range: convergence controls only a tail, and the part
outside that tail need not be finite. For example, index a net by
$\mathbb R$ in its usual order, put $x_t=-t$ for $t<0$ and $x_t=0$
for $t\geq0$. It converges in norm to zero but its range is unbounded.

\begin{proposition}[A weakly null net with no bounded tail]
\label{prop:weak_net_unbounded_tails}
In an infinite-dimensional normed space $E$, there is a net converging
weakly to zero such that every tail is unbounded in norm. No simultaneous
choice of vectors and no Hahn--Banach theorem is needed.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
Let $I$ consist of triples $(F,n,u)$ where $F$ is a finite subset of
$E'$, $n$ is a positive integer, and $u$ is a unit vector in the common
kernel of $F$. Such a vector exists for each finite $F$ by the finite
linear-dependence argument used for weak neighbourhoods. Include all
these vectors in the index set, rather than selecting one for each $F$.
For the empty $F$, the common kernel is $E$.

Order triples by equality or by
\[
(F,n,u)<(G,m,v)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\subseteq G\ \text{and}\ n<m.
\]
This is a partial order. Two triples have an upper bound: use their
union of functionals, an integer larger than both integers, and any
one unit vector in the resulting common kernel. This is a single
existence argument for each pair of indices, not a choice function.

Define $x_{(F,n,u)}=nu$. Given $\ell\in E'$, an index with
$F=\{\ell\}$ exists, and every later index annihilates $\ell$.
Thus the net converges weakly to zero. Above a fixed $(F,n,u)$ lie
all $(F,m,u)$ with $m>n$, whose vectors have norm $m$. Every tail
is therefore unbounded. The construction is a function on the explicit
set of all triples and makes no simultaneous selections.
\Done

For each individual functional, the scalar values in this example vanish
on a suitable tail. Those tails depend on the functional; they do not
supply the common pointwise-bounded family needed to apply uniform
boundedness. In particular, weak convergence of a general net does not
imply even eventual norm boundedness.

\subsection{Open mappings and closed graphs}
\begin{theorem}[Open mapping theorem]
\label{thm:open_mapping}
Let $E,F$ be Banach spaces and let $T:E\to F$ be bounded, linear,
and surjective. Then $T$ maps open sets to open sets.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Write $\overline B_E=\{x:\|x\|\leq1\}$. Surjectivity gives
\[
F=\bigcup_{n\geq1}T(n\overline B_E)
 \subseteq\bigcup_{n\geq1}\overline{T(n\overline B_E)}=F.
\]
By Baire's theorem, one of the closed sets on the right contains an
open ball $B_F(y_0,r)$, with $r>0$. Subtracting its centre gives
\[
B_F(0,r)\subseteq
\overline{T(n\overline B_E)}-\overline{T(n\overline B_E)}
\subseteq\overline{T(2n\overline B_E)}.
\]
For the last inclusion, approximate the two terms by image points,
subtract their preimages, and use the triangle inequality. Scaling
therefore gives a number $\delta>0$ such that
\[
B_F(0,\delta)\subseteq\overline{T(\overline B_E)}.
\]
This is only a closure statement; the next step removes the closure.

Fix $y$ with $\|y\|<\delta/2$. By the scaled closure inclusion,
choose $x_1$ with $\|x_1\|\leq1/2$ and
$\|y-Tx_1\|<\delta/4$. Inductively, if the residual after $k-1$
terms has norm less than $\delta2^{-k}$, choose $x_k$ with
\[
\|x_k\|\leq2^{-k},\qquad
\left\|y-T\sum_{j=1}^k x_j\right\|<\delta2^{-(k+1)}.
\]
Completeness of $E$ makes the series converge to $x$ with $\|x\|\leq1$.
Continuity of $T$ and the residual estimate give $Tx=y$. Hence
$B_F(0,\delta/2)\subseteq T(\overline B_E)$. Scaling once more yields
\[
B_F(0,\delta/4)\subseteq T(\tfrac12\overline B_E)
\subseteq T(B_E(0,1)).
\]
Translation and scaling give, for every $a>0$,
\[
B_F(Tx_0,a\delta/4)\subseteq T(B_E(x_0,a)).
\]
Every open neighbourhood of $x_0$ contains one of these domain balls.
Taking such balls at every point proves openness. Completeness of $F$
was used in Baire's theorem, and completeness of $E$ in summing the lifts.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Bounded inverse theorem]
\label{thm:bounded_inverse}
A bounded linear bijection between Banach spaces has a bounded linear
inverse.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The inverse is linear by bijectivity and linearity of the original map.
Openness of the original map says precisely that its inverse is
continuous. The bounded-map lemma then gives boundedness.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Closed graph theorem]
\label{thm:closed_graph}
Let $E,F$ be Banach spaces and let $T:E\to F$ be an everywhere-defined
linear map. Then $T$ is bounded if and only if its graph
$G=\{(x,Tx):x\in E\}$ is closed in $E\times F$.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
If $T$ is bounded and $(x_n,Tx_n)\to(x,y)$, continuity gives
$Tx_n\to Tx$, so $y=Tx$. This proves closedness, since the product
is metrizable. Conversely, equip $E\times F$ with the norm
$\|(x,y)\|=\|x\|+\|y\|$. Coordinatewise convergence of Cauchy
sequences proves its completeness. A closed subspace is complete,
so $G$ is Banach. The projection $p:G\to E$, $(x,Tx)\mapsto x$,
is a bounded linear bijection; surjectivity uses that $T$ is defined
on all of $E$. The bounded inverse theorem makes $p^{-1}$ bounded.
The second-coordinate projection $q:G\to F$ is bounded, and
$T=q\circ p^{-1}$ is therefore bounded.
\Done

\begin{example}[Why completeness and the domain matter]
Give the vector space $\ell^1$ first its usual norm and then the
supremum norm. The identity from the first normed space to the second
is bounded and bijective, but its inverse is unbounded: a vector with
$n$ coordinates equal to one has supremum norm one and $\ell^1$ norm
$n$. The target is incomplete. The finite truncations of $(1/k)_{k\geq1}$
are Cauchy in supremum norm but have no supremum-norm limit in $\ell^1$.
This does not contradict the bounded inverse theorem.

For the domain restriction, on $\ell^2$ put
\[
D(T)=\{x\in\ell^2:(n x_n)_{n\geq1}\in\ell^2\},
\qquad (Tx)_n=n x_n.
\]
Its graph is closed: if $x^{(k)}\to x$ and $Tx^{(k)}\to y$ in
$\ell^2$, coordinate convergence gives $y_n=nx_n$; since $y\in\ell^2$,
this means $x\in D(T)$ and $Tx=y$. Nevertheless
$\|Te_n\|=n$ while $\|e_n\|=1$, so $T$ is unbounded for the inherited
$\ell^2$ norm on its domain. The domain is proper: $(1/n)_{n\geq1}$
belongs to $\ell^2$ but its image would be the constant sequence one.
Its finite truncations belong to $D(T)$ and converge to it in $\ell^2$,
so $D(T)$ is not complete for its inherited norm.
Thus closedness of a graph cannot replace the everywhere-defined
hypothesis of the theorem. The graph norm $\|x\|+\|Tx\|$ does make
$D(T)$ Banach, by its isometric identification with the closed graph.
\end{example}

\subsection{Compact operators and norm approximation}
Let $E,F$ be normed spaces over the same scalar field. Write
$B_E=\{x\in E:\|x\|\leq1\}$ for the closed unit ball.
A bounded linear map $T:E\to F$ is \emph{compact} if
$\overline{T(B_E)}$, with closure taken in $F$, is norm compact.
For bounded linear maps use the operator norm
\[
\|T\|=\sup_{x\in B_E}\|Tx\|.
\]
The bounded-map lemma and scaling give
$\|Tx\|\leq\|T\|\,\|x\|$. The metric compactness criterion and
its classical countable-choice convention continue to apply here.

\begin{proposition}[Sequential characterization of compact operators]
\label{prop:compact_operator_sequences}
A bounded linear map $T:E\to F$ is compact if and only if every
bounded sequence $(x_n)$ in $E$ has a subsequence whose images
$Tx_{n_k}$ converge in norm to an element of $F$.
Neither space needs to be complete.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
If $T$ is compact and $\|x_n\|\leq R$ for some $R>0$, the images
belong to $R\overline{T(B_E)}$, a compact set. The metric compactness
criterion gives a convergent subsequence.
Conversely, let $(y_n)$ be any sequence in $\overline{T(B_E)}$.
Choose $x_n\in B_E$ with $\|Tx_n-y_n\|<1/n$. The assumed property
gives $Tx_{n_k}\to y$ in $F$, hence $y_{n_k}\to y$ as well.
Closedness places $y$ in $\overline{T(B_E)}$. This closure is
sequentially compact and therefore compact by the metric criterion.
The approximations $x_n$ use countable choice.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Finite rank and the identity]
Every bounded linear map with finite-dimensional range is compact.
The identity on a normed space is compact if and only if that space
is finite-dimensional.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The linear-algebra chapter's result on finite-dimensional ranges says
exactly that the image of the unit ball has compact closure. For the
identity, the image is the closed unit ball itself, so its compactness
is equivalent to finite dimension by the compact-unit-ball theorem
in that chapter. Neither assertion requires ambient completeness.
\Done

\begin{proposition}[Sums and compositions]
The compact maps $E\to F$ form a vector subspace of the bounded
linear maps. If $T:E\to F$ is compact and $A:D\to E$ and
$B:F\to G$ are bounded linear maps between normed spaces, then
$BTA:D\to G$ is compact.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
For compact $S,T$ and a bounded sequence in $E$, first extract a
subsequence on which the $S$ images converge, and from it one on
which the $T$ images converge. Every fixed linear combination of
the two images then converges. The sequential characterization proves
closure under linear combinations; the zero map is compact too.
For the composition, $A$ sends bounded sequences to bounded sequences,
$T$ supplies a subsequence with convergent images, and continuity of
$B$ preserves that convergence. Apply the same characterization.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Norm limits of compact operators]
\label{thm:compact_operator_norm_limit}
Let $E$ be normed and $F$ Banach. If compact operators $T_n:E\to F$
converge in operator norm to a bounded linear map $T:E\to F$, then
$T$ is compact. In particular, the compact operators form a closed
subspace of the bounded linear operators in the operator norm.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $n$ with
$\|T-T_n\|<\varepsilon/4$. Compactness of
$\overline{T_n(B_E)}$ makes $T_n(B_E)$ totally bounded. Choose
$x_1,\ldots,x_m\in B_E$ whose images under $T_n$ form a finite
$\varepsilon/4$-net for that image. For every $x\in B_E$, some
$j$ satisfies
\[
\begin{split}
\|Tx-Tx_j\|
&\leq\|(T-T_n)x\|+\|T_nx-T_nx_j\|+\|(T_n-T)x_j\|\\
&<3\varepsilon/4<\varepsilon.
\end{split}
\]
Thus $T(B_E)$ is totally bounded. Its closure remains totally bounded
and is complete because $F$ is Banach. The metric compactness criterion
therefore makes this closure compact. Sequential closedness is
closedness in the operator-norm metric, giving the final assertion.
\Done

\begin{example}[Why completeness of the target enters]
Let $c_{00}$ denote the real or complex sequences with finite support,
equipped with the $\ell^2$ norm. Take $E=F=c_{00}$ and define
\[
(Tx)_j=x_j/j,\qquad
(T_mx)_j=\begin{cases}x_j/j,&j\leq m,\\0,&j>m.\end{cases}
\]
These maps preserve finite support. Each $T_m$ is bounded and has
finite rank, hence is compact. Directly from the norm formula,
\[
\|(T-T_m)x\|_2\leq\frac{\|x\|_2}{m+1},\qquad
\|T-T_m\|=\frac1{m+1},
\]
where equality is obtained at the coordinate vector $e_{m+1}$.
Nevertheless $T$ is not compact. Put
$x^{(N)}_j=1/(2j)$ for $j\leq N$ and zero otherwise. These vectors
belong to $B_E$, since
$\sum_{j\geq1}j^{-2}\leq2$ (for $j\geq2$, compare with
$1/(j(j-1))$ and telescope). Every norm-convergent subsequence of
$Tx^{(N)}$ would converge coordinatewise to the sequence
$(1/(2j^2))_{j\geq1}$, which has infinite support and is not in $F$.
There is therefore no such subsequence in $F$, contradicting the
sequential characterization if $T$ were compact.
The target is indeed incomplete: $(x^{(N)})$ is Cauchy by convergence
of $\sum j^{-2}$, but a limit in $c_{00}$ would have coordinates
$1/(2j)$ for every $j$, which is impossible.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Compact operators on weakly convergent sequences]
\label{prop:compact_weak_to_norm}
If $T:E\to F$ is compact and $x_n\to x$ weakly in $E$, then
$Tx_n\to Tx$ in norm. The spaces need not be Banach; this assertion
uses the earlier Hahn--Banach consequences for normed spaces.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
The weakly convergent sequence is norm bounded. Suppose its images do
not converge in norm to $Tx$. Then some subsequence satisfies
$\|Tx_{n_k}-Tx\|\geq\varepsilon$ for a fixed $\varepsilon>0$.
Compactness gives a further subsequence converging in norm to some
$y\in F$. For every $\ell\in F'$, the functional $\ell\circ T$
belongs to $E'$, so weak convergence gives
$\ell(Tx_{n_k})\to\ell(Tx)$ along this further subsequence.
Norm convergence gives the limit $\ell(y)$ as well. The continuous
dual separates points by Hahn--Banach, hence $y=Tx$, contradicting
the distance bound. Thus the whole image sequence converges in norm.
\Done

\subsection{The Schur property of $\ell^1$}
For $\mathbb K=\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, put
\[
\ell^1(\mathbb K)=
\left\{x=(x_j)_{j\geq1}:\sum_{j=1}^\infty|x_j|<\infty\right\},
\qquad \|x\|_1=\sum_{j=1}^\infty|x_j|.
\]
The scalar triangle inequality makes this a normed vector space.
Convergence of the nonnegative series implies
$\sum_{j>m}|x_j|\to0$ for each $x\in\ell^1$.
This space is also $L^1$ for counting measure on the positive integers:
integration is summation, by monotone convergence applied to finite
truncations. Counting measure has infinite total mass. The theorem
below therefore concerns a different measure setting from the
finite-measure Dunford--Pettis criterion that follows.

For a bounded scalar sequence $b=(b_j)$ the formula
\[
L_b(x)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty b_jx_j
\]
defines a continuous linear functional on $\ell^1$. Indeed, the series
converges absolutely and
$|L_b(x)|\leq(\sup_j|b_j|)\|x\|_1$; absolute convergence justifies
termwise addition and scalar multiplication. Coordinate evaluation is
a special case, so weak convergence implies convergence of each
coordinate. We only need these explicit functionals, not a theorem
identifying every member of the dual.

\begin{theorem}[Schur's theorem]
\label{thm:schur_ell1}
A sequence in real or complex $\ell^1$ converges weakly to $x$ if
and only if it converges to $x$ in norm.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Norm convergence implies weak convergence in every normed space.
For the converse, subtract the weak limit and write the resulting
weakly null sequence as $(y_n)$. Suppose its norms do not tend to
zero. There is $\varepsilon>0$ such that infinitely many indices
satisfy $\|y_n\|_1\geq\varepsilon$.

We construct increasing indices $n_k$ and increasing nonnegative
integers $m_k$, starting with $n_0=m_0=0$. Having chosen $m_{k-1}$,
coordinatewise convergence to zero ensures that, for all sufficiently
large $n$,
\[
\sum_{j\leq m_{k-1}}|(y_n)_j|<\varepsilon/8.
\]
Choose $n_k>n_{k-1}$ among the infinitely many indices
with norm at least $\varepsilon$, also satisfying that prefix bound,
and set $z_k=y_{n_k}$. Since $z_k\in\ell^1$, choose
$m_k>m_{k-1}$ so that its tail satisfies
\[
\sum_{j\leq m_{k-1}}|(z_k)_j|<\varepsilon/8,
\qquad
\sum_{j>m_k}|(z_k)_j|<\varepsilon/8.
\]
Each integer choice may be made as the least admissible integer;
the recursion requires no simultaneous selection of vectors.
The mass on the intervening block therefore satisfies
\[
\sum_{m_{k-1}<j\leq m_k}|(z_k)_j|>3\varepsilon/4.
\]

The disjoint blocks $(m_{k-1},m_k]$ cover the positive integers,
since $m_k\geq k$. Define a single coefficient sequence by
\[
b_j=\begin{cases}
\overline{(z_k)_j}/|(z_k)_j|,&
 m_{k-1}<j\leq m_k\text{ and }(z_k)_j\ne0,\\
0,&m_{k-1}<j\leq m_k\text{ and }(z_k)_j=0.
\end{cases}
\]
In the real case the first expression is the usual sign.
We have $|b_j|\leq1$, so $L_b$ is a continuous linear functional.
On the $k$th block, $b_j(z_k)_j=|(z_k)_j|$; outside that block,
the total absolute contribution is less than $\varepsilon/4$.
Consequently
\[
\operatorname{Re}L_b(z_k)
\geq\sum_{m_{k-1}<j\leq m_k}|(z_k)_j|
 -\sum_{j\leq m_{k-1}}|(z_k)_j|
 -\sum_{j>m_k}|(z_k)_j|
>\varepsilon/2.
\]
Here $\operatorname{Re}$ is redundant over the reals. This contradicts
weak nullity, which requires $L_b(z_k)\to0$. Hence
$\|y_n\|_1\to0$, proving the theorem.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Weak-to-norm preservation does not imply compactness]
The identity on $\ell^1$ sends every weakly convergent sequence to a
norm-convergent sequence, but is not compact.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
The first assertion is Schur's theorem. For the second, the coordinate
vectors $e_j$ form a bounded sequence with
$\|e_j-e_k\|_1=2$ whenever $j\ne k$. Their images under the identity
have no norm-convergent subsequence, so the sequential compact-operator
criterion excludes compactness.
\Done

The vectors $e_j$ do converge coordinatewise to zero, but they do not
converge weakly to zero: the bounded coefficient sequence $b_j=1$
gives $L_b(e_j)=1$. Thus coordinate tests alone would miss exactly
the obstruction detected by the block functional in the proof.

Schur's theorem is a statement about sequences, not equality of
topologies. The space $\ell^1$ is infinite-dimensional, so its weak
neighbourhoods of zero contain unbounded linear subspaces and its weak topology
differs from its norm topology, as proved earlier. Proposition~
\ref{prop:weak_net_unbounded_tails} even gives a weakly null net in
$\ell^1$ with every tail norm-unbounded. The sequence result thus does
not extend to arbitrary nets. Nor does it hold in every $L^1$ space:
the Rademacher sequence on $[0,1)$ in the probability chapter is weakly
null in $L^1$ and has norm one throughout. Infinite counting measure
and a finite nonatomic measure produce different examples; the
finite-measure compactness criterion below does not identify these
settings.

A set is relatively weakly compact when its closure in the weak
topology is compact. We first prove a sequential criterion: every sequence
from the family has a subsequence converging weakly to an element of the
ambient space. The limit need not belong to the original family. A later
argument proves the topological compactness statement separately, rather
than assuming that sequences characterize an arbitrary weak topology.

\subsection{A finite-measure Dunford--Pettis criterion}
Fix a finite positive measure space $(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ and
use the real space $L^1(\mu)$. A family $K\subseteq L^1(\mu)$ is
\emph{uniformly integrable} in the convention used here when
\[
\lim_{M\to\infty}\sup_{f\in K}
 \int_{\{|f|>M\}}|f|\,d\mu=0.
\]
For the empty family the condition is taken to hold. The formula is
independent of the measurable representatives of its members.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform small-set control]
For a family $K\subseteq L^1(\mu)$ on this finite measure space,
uniform integrability is equivalent to the conjunction of
\[
\sup_{f\in K}\|f\|_1<\infty
\]
and the following condition: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is
$\delta>0$ such that
\[
\mu(A)<\delta\implies
\sup_{f\in K}\int_A|f|\,d\mu<\varepsilon
\qquad(A\in\mathcal{A}).
\]
\end{lemma}
\Proof
Assume uniform integrability. Choosing a positive $M$ whose tail
integrals are at most one gives
$\|f\|_1\leqslant M\mu(\Omega)+1$ for all $f\in K$.
Given $\varepsilon>0$, instead choose $M>0$ with all tail integrals
less than $\varepsilon/2$. Then
\[
\int_A|f|\,d\mu
\leqslant M\mu(A)+\int_{\{|f|>M\}}|f|\,d\mu
<\varepsilon
\]
whenever $\mu(A)<\varepsilon/(2M)$.

Conversely, let $C$ bound the norms and choose $\delta$ for the
small-set condition. For $M>0$,
$\mu(\{|f|>M\})\leqslant C/M$ for every $f\in K$.
Choosing $M>C/\delta$ makes each such set small enough that its
integral is less than $\varepsilon$. This is uniform integrability.
The empty family satisfies both conditions by convention.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Vitali convergence theorem]
\label{thm:vitali_finite}
Suppose $f_n\in L^1(\mu)$ and $f$ is a measurable real function, the family $\{f_n:n\geqslant1\}$ is
uniformly integrable, and $f_n\to f$ in measure, meaning
\[
\mu(\{|f_n-f|>a\})\longrightarrow0\quad\text{for every }a>0.
\]
Then $f\in L^1(\mu)$ and $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
The small-set lemma gives $C=\sup_n\|f_n\|_1<\infty$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:measure_subsequence}, choose a subsequence converging
to $f$ almost everywhere. Fatou's lemma gives
\[
\int_\Omega|f|\,d\mu\leqslant
\liminf_k\int_\Omega|f_{n_k}|\,d\mu\leqslant C.
\]
Thus integrability of the limit is a conclusion, not an assumption.
An individual integrable function has vanishing tail integrals, so
adjoining $f$ preserves uniform integrability. By the lemma, for a
given $\varepsilon>0$ choose $\delta>0$ such that
$\mu(A)<\delta$ implies both
$\int_A|f_n|\,d\mu<\varepsilon/3$ for every $n$ and
$\int_A|f|\,d\mu<\varepsilon/3$.
Choose $a>0$ with $a\mu(\Omega)<\varepsilon/3$.
For all sufficiently large $n$, the set
$A_n=\{|f_n-f|>a\}$ has measure less than $\delta$. Hence
\[
\|f_n-f\|_1
\leqslant a\mu(\Omega)+\int_{A_n}|f_n|\,d\mu
                   +\int_{A_n}|f|\,d\mu
<\varepsilon.
\]
This proves norm convergence.
\Done

The proof uses Fatou's lemma and the basic Lebesgue-integral fact that
an integrable function has vanishing tails. A related presentation is
\href{https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~bdriver/280_06-07/Lecture_Notes/N23_2p.pdf}
{B. Driver, \emph{Probability Theory}, Theorem~11.31}.
The theorem just proved yields weak convergence as well, by the norm
estimate for the $L^\infty$ pairing. It strengthens the next criterion
when convergence in measure is already known: the whole sequence then
converges in norm, rather than merely possessing weakly convergent
subsequences.

\begin{proposition}[The converse and an $L^1$ convergence criterion]
On the finite measure space above, if $f_n\to f$ in $L^1$, then
$f_n\to f$ in measure and $\{f_n:n\geqslant1\}$ is uniformly integrable.
Consequently, for measurable real $f$ and $f_n\in L^1$, convergence
in measure together with uniform integrability is equivalent to
$f\in L^1$ and convergence to $f$ in $L^1$.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
For $a>0$, the inequality
$a\mu(\{|f_n-f|>a\})\leqslant\|f_n-f\|_1$ proves convergence
in measure. The sequence of norms is bounded by the triangle inequality.
To prove uniform small-set control, fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$
so that $\|f_n-f\|_1<\varepsilon/2$ for $n\geqslant N$.
The finite family $f,f_1,\ldots,f_{N-1}$ has uniformly small integrals
on sets of sufficiently small measure: for each member this follows
by splitting at a large height, and one takes the minimum of finitely
many positive bounds for the measure. Choose such a bound so that
each integral is less than $\varepsilon/2$. Then, for $n\geqslant N$,
\[
\int_A|f_n|\,d\mu\leqslant\|f_n-f\|_1+\int_A|f|\,d\mu
<\varepsilon;
\]
the finitely many earlier terms satisfy the same conclusion.
The small-set lemma proves uniform integrability. The reverse
implication is Theorem~\ref{thm:vitali_finite}.
\Done

\begin{corollary}[Domination with convergence in measure]
Let $f_n$ and $f$ be measurable real functions. If
$|f_n|\leqslant h$ almost everywhere for one $h\in L^1$, and
$f_n\to f$ in measure on this finite measure space, then
$f\in L^1$ and $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$.
\end{corollary}
\Proof
Replace $h$ by $|h|$ if necessary. The bound
\[
\int_{\{|f_n|>M\}}|f_n|\,d\mu
\leqslant\int_{\{h>M\}}h\,d\mu
\]
gives uniform integrability; apply the theorem. In particular,
convergence almost everywhere implies the conclusion here, since
on a finite measure space the decreasing sets
$\bigcup_{n\geqslant N}\{|f_n-f|>a\}$ have measures tending to zero
for every $a>0$. This last argument uses continuity of measure from
above and is precisely where finiteness enters.
\Done

\subsection{Stability of uniform integrability}
The finite-measure setting continues throughout this subsection. These
facts distinguish norm compactness from the weak subsequence property
in Theorem~\ref{thm:DP_sequential_finite}.

\begin{proposition}[Closure and convex combinations]
If $K\subseteq L^1(\mu)$ is uniformly integrable, then its norm-closed
convex hull is uniformly integrable. More generally, the same is true
for the norm closure of all finite sums $\sum_{j=1}^m a_jf_j$, where
$f_j\in K$, $a_j\in\mathbb R$, and $\sum_j|a_j|\leqslant1$.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
Let $C$ be a bound for the norms of members of $K$. Every such sum has
norm at most $C$, and for any measurable $A$,
\[
\int_A\left|\sum_j a_jf_j\right|\,d\mu
 \leqslant\sum_j|a_j|\int_A|f_j|\,d\mu
 \leqslant\sup_{f\in K}\int_A|f|\,d\mu.
\]
The uniform small-set condition therefore passes to these sums.
It also passes to their norm closure: for a norm limit $g_n\to g$,
\[
\left|\int_A|g_n|\,d\mu-\int_A|g|\,d\mu\right|
\leqslant\|g_n-g\|_1.
\]
For a prescribed $\varepsilon>0$, choose the original small-set bound
with $\varepsilon/2$; limits then have integrals at most
$\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$. Norm bounds likewise pass to limits.
Apply the small-set characterization. The empty-family case is
immediate, taking the allowed empty sum to be zero.
\Done

\begin{proposition}[Norm compactness implies uniform integrability]
Every relatively norm-compact subset $K\subseteq L^1(\mu)$ is
uniformly integrable.
\end{proposition}
\Proof
Its norm closure is compact and hence bounded: the open balls centered
at zero with positive integer radii cover it, and a finite subcover
supplies a bound. For $\varepsilon>0$, compactness also gives a finite
$\varepsilon/2$-net $g_1,\ldots,g_m$ in $L^1$. The finite family has
uniformly small integrals on small measurable sets, by the same
finite-minimum argument used in the converse Vitali theorem. Choose
$\delta>0$ so that $\mu(A)<\delta$ implies
$\int_A|g_j|\,d\mu<\varepsilon/2$ for every $j$. For any $f\in K$,
choose $j$ with $\|f-g_j\|_1<\varepsilon/2$. Then
\[
\int_A|f|\,d\mu\leqslant\|f-g_j\|_1+\int_A|g_j|\,d\mu
<\varepsilon.
\]
The characterization proves uniform integrability.
\Done

The converse is false. The Rademacher functions in the probability
section are bounded by one and hence uniformly integrable, but any
two distinct ones have $L^1$ distance one: they disagree on a set of
measure $1/2$, where their difference has absolute value two. Thus
no subsequence is norm Cauchy. Their weak convergence is compatible
with this failure of norm compactness.

\subsection{Hilbert representation and the dual of real $L^1$}
\begin{theorem}[Representation of a real Hilbert-space functional]
\label{thm:real_Hilbert_representation}
Let $H$ be a real Hilbert space. For each continuous linear functional
$\ell:H\to\mathbb R$ there is a unique $g\in H$ such that
\[
\ell(x)=\langle x,g\rangle\quad(x\in H),\qquad \|\ell\|=\|g\|.
\]
\end{theorem}
\Proof
For $\ell=0$, take $g=0$. Otherwise the closed affine set
$S=\{z:\ell(z)=1\}$ is nonempty. Its distance $d$ from zero satisfies
$d\geq1/\|\ell\|>0$. Choose $z_n\in S$ with $\|z_n\|\to d$.
Since $(z_n+z_m)/2\in S$, the parallelogram identity gives
\[
\|z_n-z_m\|^2
=2\|z_n\|^2+2\|z_m\|^2-4\|(z_n+z_m)/2\|^2
\leq2\|z_n\|^2+2\|z_m\|^2-4d^2.
\]
Thus $(z_n)$ is Cauchy. Completeness and closedness give a limit
$z\in S$ of norm $d$. For $v\in\ker\ell$ and every real $t$,
$z+tv\in S$, so
$0\leq2t\langle z,v\rangle+t^2\|v\|^2$.
Considering positive and negative sufficiently small $t$ shows
$\langle z,v\rangle=0$.

For each $x$, the vector $x-\ell(x)z$ lies in the kernel. Hence
$\langle x,z\rangle=\ell(x)\|z\|^2$, and $g=z/\|z\|^2$ gives
the desired representation. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\|\ell\|\leq\|g\|$;
testing on $g/\|g\|$ gives the reverse inequality when $g\ne0$.
If $g'$ is another representing vector, testing their difference on
$x=g-g'$ gives $\|g-g'\|^2=0$, proving uniqueness.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[The dual of real $L^1$ on a finite measure space]
\label{thm:L1_dual_finite}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$ be a finite positive measure space.
The map
\[
L^\infty(\mu)\longrightarrow (L^1(\mu))',\qquad
 g\longmapsto\left(f\longmapsto\int fg\,d\mu\right)
\]
is a linear isometric bijection, with functions identified almost everywhere.
No completeness assumption on the measure is needed.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
For $g\in L^\infty$, the integral defines a bounded functional
$\ell_g$ with $\|\ell_g\|\leq\|g\|_\infty$.
If $0\leq r<\|g\|_\infty$, the measurable set $A=\{|g|>r\}$ has
positive, finite measure. Testing on
$f=\operatorname{sgn}(g)\mathbf1_A/\mu(A)$ gives $\|f\|_1=1$ and
$\ell_g(f)>r$. Letting $r$ increase to $\|g\|_\infty$ proves norm
equality; for $g=0$ it is immediate. This also proves injectivity.

For surjectivity let $\ell\in(L^1)'$ and put $C=\|\ell\|$.
Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$\|f\|_1\leq\mu(\Omega)^{1/2}\|f\|_2$, so restriction of $\ell$
to $L^2$ is continuous. Theorem~\ref{thm:L2_complete} and real Hilbert
representation supply $g\in L^2$ with
$\ell(h)=\int hg\,d\mu$ for every $h\in L^2$.
For $\varepsilon>0$, put $A_\varepsilon=\{|g|>C+\varepsilon\}$.
The function $h=\operatorname{sgn}(g)\mathbf1_{A_\varepsilon}$ is in
$L^2$ and $L^1$ because the measure is finite. Therefore
\[
(C+\varepsilon)\mu(A_\varepsilon)
\leq\int_{A_\varepsilon}|g|\,d\mu
=\ell(h)\leq C\|h\|_1=C\mu(A_\varepsilon).
\]
It follows that $\mu(A_\varepsilon)=0$. Taking the union over
$\varepsilon=1/n$ shows $|g|\leq C$ almost everywhere, so
$g\in L^\infty$.

For any $f\in L^1$, its bounded truncations
$f_m=\max(-m,\min(f,m))$ belong to $L^2$ and converge in $L^1$ to $f$,
by the vanishing integrable-tail lemma. Continuity of $\ell$ and
$|\int(f-f_m)g\,d\mu|\leq C\|f-f_m\|_1$ now give
$\ell(f)=\int fg\,d\mu$. This proves surjectivity and the theorem.
If $\mu(\Omega)=0$, both spaces consist of the zero equivalence class,
and the same conclusion is immediate.
\Done

In particular, convergence of all the bounded-test integrals below is
exactly weak convergence in real $L^1$, not merely convergence against
an unspecified subset of its continuous dual. The finite-measure
hypothesis enters both the inclusion $L^2\subseteq L^1$ and the use
of bounded test functions in $L^2$.

\subsection{A proved direction of the compactness criterion}
The following argument uses the Hilbert structure of $L^2(\mu)$ proved
in Theorem~\ref{thm:L2_complete}.
We supply the weak subsequence argument rather than assuming reflexive
weak compactness. The finite-measure dual identification in
Theorem~\ref{thm:L1_dual_finite} identifies convergence of all bounded
integrals with weak convergence.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded sequences in a Hilbert space]
Every bounded sequence $(u_n)$ in a Hilbert space has a weakly convergent
subsequence.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
The closed linear span $H_0$ of the sequence has a finite or countable
orthonormal basis $(e_j)$: apply Gram--Schmidt to the ordered list
$u_1,u_2,\ldots$, discarding zero remainders, and take the closed span.
If no vector remains, the sequence is zero. If $\|u_n\|\leq C$,
repeated scalar subsequence extraction followed by diagonalization makes
every coordinate converge, say
$\langle u_{n_k},e_j\rangle\to a_j$ (inner products linear in the first
argument). Finite-dimensional Pythagoras gives
$\sum_{j=1}^m|a_j|^2\leq C^2$ for every $m$.
Thus the orthogonal series $u=\sum_j a_je_j$ converges by completeness
and has norm at most $C$. Coordinate convergence gives convergence
against finite linear combinations of the $e_j$. Approximating any
vector of $H_0$ in norm by such combinations, and using
$\|u_{n_k}-u\|\leq2C$, gives convergence against all of $H_0$.
For an arbitrary $y$ in the Hilbert space, Bessel's inequality constructs
$y_0=\sum_j\langle y,e_j\rangle e_j$; its difference from $y$ is
orthogonal to every $e_j$ and hence to $H_0$. Pairing against $y$ is
therefore the same as pairing against $y_0$ for these vectors.

To obtain weak convergence in the sense of \emph{all} continuous linear
functionals, let $\ell$ be such a functional and set $b_j=\ell(e_j)$.
For $v_m=\sum_{j=1}^m\overline{b_j}e_j$, boundedness gives
\[
\sum_{j=1}^m|b_j|^2=|\ell(v_m)|\leq\|\ell\|\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^m|b_j|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}.
\]
Thus $\sum_j|b_j|^2\leq\|\ell\|^2$. In the real case conjugation is
omitted. On $H_0$, norm convergence of orthonormal expansions and
continuity imply $\ell(v)=\sum_j\langle v,e_j\rangle b_j$.
The tail after $m$ is bounded by
$\|v\|(\sum_{j>m}|b_j|^2)^{1/2}$ by Cauchy--Schwarz.
Apply this bound to $v=u_{n_k}-u$, whose norm is at most $2C$;
the finite part tends to zero by coordinate convergence, and the tail
bound tends to zero with $m$. Therefore $\ell(u_{n_k})\to\ell(u)$.
This proves weak convergence without invoking Riesz representation.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Uniform integrability gives weak subsequences]
\label{thm:UI_weak_subsequence}
On the finite measure space under consideration, every uniformly
integrable sequence $(f_n)$ in real $L^1$ has a subsequence and an
$f\in L^1$ such that
\[
\int h f_{n_k}\,d\mu\longrightarrow\int h f\,d\mu
\qquad(h\in L^\infty).
\]
\end{theorem}
\Proof
For positive integers $m$ let $T_m(t)=\max(-m,\min(t,m))$ and put
\[
\varepsilon_m=\sup_n\|f_n-T_m(f_n)\|_1.
\]
Uniform integrability implies $\varepsilon_m\to0$, since
$|t-T_m(t)|\leq |t|\mathbf1_{\{|t|>m\}}$.
For each fixed $m$, the functions $T_m(f_n)$ have $L^2$ norm at most
$m\mu(\Omega)^{1/2}$. Apply the Hilbert lemma successively for
$m=1,2,\ldots$ and take a diagonal subsequence. It gives functions
$g_m\in L^2$ such that $T_m(f_{n_k})$ converges weakly in $L^2$ to
$g_m$ for every fixed $m$.

For $m,l$, the triangle inequality gives
\[
\|T_m(f_{n_k})-T_l(f_{n_k})\|_1\leq\varepsilon_m+\varepsilon_l.
\]
The bounded measurable function $h=\operatorname{sgn}(g_m-g_l)$
belongs to $L^2$ because the measure is finite. Test both weak limits
against this $h$ to obtain
\[
\|g_m-g_l\|_1
=\lim_k\int h\bigl(T_m(f_{n_k})-T_l(f_{n_k})\bigr)\,d\mu
\leq\varepsilon_m+\varepsilon_l.
\]
Here $g_m\in L^1$ follows from Cauchy--Schwarz and finite measure.
Completeness of $L^1$ now gives $g_m\to f$ in $L^1$.
Finally fix $h\in L^\infty$, which also belongs to $L^2$. For fixed $m$,
\[
\begin{split}
\left|\int h(f_{n_k}-f)\,d\mu\right|
&\leq \|h\|_\infty\varepsilon_m
 +\left|\int h(T_m(f_{n_k})-g_m)\,d\mu\right|\\
&\quad+\|h\|_\infty\|g_m-f\|_1.
\end{split}
\]
First let $k\to\infty$ and then $m\to\infty$. The bound tends to zero,
proving the result. If the measure is zero all spaces here consist of
the zero equivalence class and the conclusion is immediate.
\Done

\subsection{The converse: small sets and the measure algebra}
The reverse implication can be proved directly from Baire's theorem,
without importing the full Vitali--Hahn--Saks theorem. The auxiliary
complete metric space consists of measurable sets modulo null differences.

\begin{lemma}[Completeness of the finite measure algebra]
\label{lem:finite_measure_algebra_complete}
On $\mathcal A$ identify $A$ and $B$ when $\mu(A\mathbin\triangle B)=0$.
The quotient has a complete metric
\[
d([A],[B])=\mu(A\mathbin\triangle B).
\]
For every $h\in L^1(\mu)$, the function
$[A]\mapsto\int_Ah\,d\mu$ is well defined and continuous.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
The triangle inequality follows from
$A\mathbin\triangle C\subseteq(A\mathbin\triangle B)\cup
(B\mathbin\triangle C)$; null differences make the metric independent
of representatives. Given a Cauchy sequence, select a subsequence with
representatives $A_k$ such that
$\mu(A_k\mathbin\triangle A_{k+1})<2^{-k}$.
Let $A=\liminf_k A_k$, a measurable set. For each $k$,
\[
A_k\mathbin\triangle A
\subseteq\bigcup_{j\geq k}(A_j\mathbin\triangle A_{j+1}).
\]
Indeed, if membership never changes after stage $k$, membership in the
liminf agrees with membership at that stage. Thus
$d([A_k],[A])\leq\sum_{j\geq k}2^{-j}\to0$.
The original Cauchy sequence converges to the same limit by the triangle
inequality. Finally,
\[
\left|\int_Ah-\int_Bh\right|
\leq\int_{A\mathbin\triangle B}|h|,
\]
and absolute continuity of the integral proves continuity. These arguments
use measurable representatives throughout, not completeness of the measure.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Weak convergence gives uniform integrability]
\label{thm:weak_sequence_UI}
If $f_n$ converges weakly to $f$ in real $L^1(\mu)$, with $\mu$ finite,
then $\{f_n:n\geq1\}$ is uniformly integrable.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
Put $h_n=f_n-f$ and $\nu_n(A)=\int_Ah_n\,d\mu$.
Weak convergence against indicators gives $\nu_n(A)\to0$ for every
measurable $A$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and, in the complete measure algebra,
put
\[
E_N=\{[A]:|\nu_n(A)|\leq\varepsilon
                  \text{ for every }n\geq N\}.
\]
Each $E_N$ is closed by Lemma~\ref{lem:finite_measure_algebra_complete},
and their union is the
whole measure algebra by pointwise convergence. Baire's theorem supplies
$N$, a measurable representative $A_0$, and $\delta>0$ such that the
open $\delta$-ball about $[A_0]$ is contained in $E_N$.

If $\mu(B)<\delta$, both $[A_0\setminus B]$ and $[A_0\cup B]$
are in this ball, as is $[A_0]$. Additivity gives, for $n\geq N$,
\[
|\nu_n(B\cap A_0)|\leq2\varepsilon,
\qquad |\nu_n(B\setminus A_0)|\leq2\varepsilon,
\]
and hence $|\nu_n(B)|\leq4\varepsilon$.
Apply this bound separately to
$B\cap\{h_n\geq0\}$ and $B\cap\{h_n<0\}$ to obtain
\[
\int_B|h_n|\,d\mu\leq8\varepsilon\qquad(n\geq N).
\]
Both subsets have measure less than $\delta$, so the same uniform bound
applies even though they depend on $n$.

For any desired tolerance $\eta>0$, begin with
$\varepsilon=\eta/16$. The finitely many functions $h_1,\ldots,h_{N-1}$
have a common small-set bound by absolute continuity of their integrals;
shrinking $\delta$ therefore gives
$\sup_n\int_B|h_n|<\eta$ whenever $\mu(B)<\delta$.
Adding the single integrable function $f$ proves the same uniform
small-set property for the $f_n$. Their norms are bounded by the earlier
weak-sequence boundedness corollary. The uniform-small-set lemma now
implies uniform integrability.
\Done

\subsection{The full sequential compactness criterion}
\begin{theorem}[Dunford--Pettis, sequential finite-measure form]
\label{thm:DP_sequential_finite}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$ be a finite positive measure space and
$K\subseteq L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$. Then $K$ is uniformly integrable
if and only if
every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging weakly in $L^1(\mu)$.
Here weak convergence means
\[
\int_\Omega gf_n\,d\mu\longrightarrow\int_\Omega gf\,d\mu
\qquad\text{for every }g\in L^\infty(\mu).
\]
\end{theorem}
\Proof
The forward implication is Theorem~\ref{thm:UI_weak_subsequence}.
Conversely, suppose the stated subsequence property holds but uniform
integrability fails. Since tail integrals decrease as the threshold
increases, there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that, for every positive integer
$n$, one can choose $f_n\in K$ with
\[
\int_{\{|f_n|>n\}}|f_n|\,d\mu>\varepsilon.
\]
(The tolerance may be halved if a supremum is not attained.)
A weakly convergent subsequence $f_{n_k}$ is uniformly integrable by
Theorem~\ref{thm:weak_sequence_UI}. Choose $M$ so that all its tail integrals above $M$
are less than $\varepsilon$. For $n_k\geq M$, its tail above $n_k$ is
no larger, a contradiction. The empty family and zero-measure case
satisfy both conditions directly.
\Done

Both directions are now proved in the real finite-measure setting,
including the needed dual identification. For comparison, the
\href{https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/courses/592/dunfordpettis.pdf}
{\emph{Notes on the Dunford--Pettis compactness criterion}, Theorem~1}
give a probability-space treatment whose converse invokes
Vitali--Hahn--Saks. The measure-algebra argument here supplies the
uniform small-set conclusion needed for this sequential result.
No equivalence of sequential and topological weak compactness has been
used in this argument. We now supply a separate route to topological
compactness in this setting.
\subsection{From uniform integrability to weak compactness}
The following arguments use the proper-ultrafilter and product-compactness
results of the topology chapter, in the full Choice setting already
specified here. A proper ultrafilter on a set $S$ never contains the empty
set; convergence to a point means that every neighbourhood, intersected
with $S$, belongs to the ultrafilter. This is distinct from the earlier
local convention that also allowed an empty filter family.

\begin{theorem}[Weak compactness of a real Hilbert ball]
\label{thm:Hilbert_ball_weak_compact}
If $H$ is a real Hilbert space and $R\geq0$, the closed ball
$B_R=\{x\in H:\|x\|\leq R\}$ is compact in the weak topology.
\end{theorem}
\Proof
For each $h\in H$, let $I_h=[-R\|h\|,R\|h\|]$ and define
\[
J:B_R\longrightarrow\prod_{h\in H}I_h,\qquad
J(x)_h=\langle h,x\rangle.
\]
The product is compact by Theorem~\ref{thm:tychonoff}, since its factors
are compact real intervals. Its subset consisting of families $v$ with
\[
v_{h+k}=v_h+v_k,\qquad v_{th}=t v_h
\quad(h,k\in H,\ t\in\mathbb R)
\]
is closed: each equation is a closed condition on finitely many real
coordinates. Such a family defines the continuous linear functional
$\ell(h)=v_h$, with $|\ell(h)|\leq R\|h\|$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:real_Hilbert_representation}, it has the form
$\ell(h)=\langle h,x\rangle$ for a unique $x\in H$ with
$\|x\|=\|\ell\|\leq R$. Thus this closed subset is exactly $J[B_R]$.

The map $J$ is injective: equality of all coordinates, tested on the
difference of two points, gives equality of the points. The weak topology
on $H$ is precisely the topology induced by these coordinates, since
real Hilbert representation supplies every continuous linear functional.
Hence $J$ is a homeomorphism onto its image. That image is a closed
subset of a compact product, so $B_R$ is weakly compact. The argument
includes $R=0$ and the zero space.
\Done

\begin{lemma}[Uniform integrability passes to weak closure]
\label{lem:UI_weak_closure}
On a finite positive measure space, the weak closure in real $L^1$ of
a uniformly integrable family is uniformly integrable.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
For a measurable set $A$, the function
$q_A(f)=\int_A|f|\,d\mu$ satisfies
\[
q_A(f)=\sup\left\{\int fg\,d\mu:
 g\in L^\infty,\ |g|\leq1\text{ a.e.},\ g\mathbf1_A=g\text{ a.e.}\right\}.
\]
The bound follows from $|g|\leq1$ on $A$; equality is attained by
$g=\operatorname{sgn}(f)\mathbf1_A$. Each displayed test is weakly
continuous, so $\{f:q_A(f)\leq r\}$ is weakly closed, being the
intersection of its closed linear half-spaces. This also applies to
$q_\Omega(f)=\|f\|_1$.

Let $W$ be the weak closure of the given family $K$. Its uniform norm
bound passes to $W$ by the closedness just proved. For $\varepsilon>0$,
choose $\delta>0$ such that $\mu(A)<\delta$ implies
$q_A(f)<\varepsilon/2$ for every $f\in K$. For each such $A$, the
weakly closed set $\{f:q_A(f)\leq\varepsilon/2\}$ contains $K$ and
therefore $W$. Thus the members of $W$ satisfy the uniform bound
$q_A(f)\leq\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$. The small-set characterization
now makes $W$ uniformly integrable. Empty families cause no exception.
\Done

\begin{lemma}[Sequences in a weakly compact set]
\label{lem:weak_compact_subsequence}
Every sequence in a weakly compact subset $K$ of a real normed space
$E$ has a weakly convergent subsequence with limit in $K$.
Completeness of $E$ is not needed for this implication.
\end{lemma}
\Proof
First, every norm-closed linear subspace $M\subseteq E$ is weakly closed.
If $x\notin M$, its distance $d$ from $M$ is positive. On $M+\mathbb R x$
the functional $\ell_0(m+tx)=t$ is well defined and satisfies
\[
|\ell_0(m+tx)|\leq d^{-1}\|m+tx\|.
\]
For $t\ne0$, this follows from $\|m+tx\|\geq |t|d$; for $t=0$
it is immediate. Hahn--Banach extends $\ell_0$ to a continuous linear
functional on $E$ that vanishes on $M$ but not on $x$. Thus $M$ is the
intersection of the kernels of the continuous functionals vanishing on
it, proving weak closedness.

Given a sequence $(x_n)$ in $K$, let $M$ be its norm-closed linear span.
Finite rational linear combinations of its terms form a countable dense
subset of $M$. If $M=\{0\}$, the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise,
normalizing the nonzero elements of that dense subset gives a countable
dense sequence $(u_j)$ in the unit sphere of $M$. Choose norm-one
functionals $\ell_j\in E'$ with $\ell_j(u_j)=1$ by Hahn--Banach.
They separate points of $M$: for $z\ne0$, choose $u_j$ within $1/2$ of
$z/\|z\|$; then $|\ell_j(z/\|z\|)|>1/2$.

The set $C=K\cap M$ is weakly compact, as a closed subset of $K$.
The map
\[
C\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{\mathbb N},\qquad
x\longmapsto(\ell_j(x))_j
\]
is continuous and injective. By the compact-to-Hausdorff theorem,
it is a homeomorphism onto its image. The countable product is
metrizable, for example by
\[
d(a,b)=\sum_{j\geq1}2^{-j}\min(1,|a_j-b_j|).
\]
Finite-coordinate conditions control the initial part of this sum,
and its uniformly small tails give the converse neighbourhood
comparison, so this metric induces the product topology.
Thus $C$, with its weak topology, is compact and metrizable. The metric
compactness criterion supplies a convergent subsequence of $(x_n)$
with limit in $C\subseteq K$, proving the claim. This argument concerns
sequences inside a weakly compact set; it does not metrize the weak
topology on all of $E$ or identify general nets with sequences.
\Done

\begin{theorem}[Dunford--Pettis, finite-measure weak compactness]
\label{thm:DP_weak_compact_finite}
Let $(\Omega,\mathcal A,\mu)$ be a finite positive measure space and
$K\subseteq L^1(\mu;\mathbb R)$. The following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $K$ is uniformly integrable;
\item its weak closure is weakly compact;
\item every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence converging weakly to an
      element of $L^1(\mu)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\Proof
The first and third conditions are equivalent by
Theorem~\ref{thm:DP_sequential_finite}. The second implies the third by
Lemma~\ref{lem:weak_compact_subsequence}, applied to the weak closure.
It remains to prove that the first implies the second.

Let $W$ be the weak closure of $K$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:UI_weak_closure},
it is uniformly integrable. If $W$ is empty, it is compact. Otherwise,
fix an arbitrary proper ultrafilter $\mathcal U$ on $W$.
Set $\varepsilon_j=2^{-j}$ and choose positive integers $m_j$ such that,
with the pointwise truncation $T_j f=\max(-m_j,\min(f,m_j))$,
\[
\sup_{f\in W}\|f-T_jf\|_1\leq\varepsilon_j.
\]
Uniform integrability gives these bounds because truncation error is
bounded by the corresponding tail integral. Almost-everywhere equal
representatives have almost-everywhere equal truncations, so $T_j$
is a well-defined map on $L^1$ equivalence classes into $L^2$. Each
$T_jf$ lies in the
$L^2$ ball of radius $m_j\mu(\Omega)^{1/2}$. This ball is weakly
compact by Theorem~\ref{thm:Hilbert_ball_weak_compact} and completeness
of $L^2$. The image ultrafilter under $T_j$ therefore converges weakly
in $L^2$ to some $u_j$ in that ball. The limit is unique because the
weak Hilbert topology is Hausdorff. No continuity of the truncation
map from weak $L^1$ to weak $L^2$ is asserted or needed: it is simply
a map used to push forward an ultrafilter.

Every $g\in L^\infty$ is in $L^2$ on the finite measure space, so the
map $h\mapsto\int gh\,d\mu$ is a continuous functional on $L^2$.
For every $f\in W$,
\[
\left|\int g(T_jf-T_kf)\,d\mu\right|
 \leq\|g\|_\infty(\varepsilon_j+\varepsilon_k).
\]
Passing to the scalar limits along $\mathcal U$ preserves this closed
inequality and gives the same bound with $u_j-u_k$ in place of
$T_jf-T_kf$. Here sums and differences of scalar ultrafilter limits
are justified by intersecting the finitely many required filter sets.
Both $u_j,u_k$ belong to $L^1$ by the finite-measure inclusion
$L^2\subseteq L^1$. Taking $g=\operatorname{sgn}(u_j-u_k)$ gives
\[
\|u_j-u_k\|_1\leq\varepsilon_j+\varepsilon_k.
\]
Completeness of $L^1$ yields $u_j\to f_*$ in norm for some $f_*\in L^1$;
letting $k$ tend to infinity in this estimate gives
$\|u_j-f_*\|_1\leq\varepsilon_j$.

For each $g\in L^\infty$ and $f\in W$,
\[
\left|\int g(f-f_*)\,d\mu\right|
\leq 2\|g\|_\infty\varepsilon_j
   +\left|\int g(T_jf-u_j)\,d\mu\right|.
\]
First choose $j$ making the first term arbitrarily small; the second
tends to zero along $\mathcal U$ by the weak $L^2$ limit. Therefore
$\int gf$ tends along $\mathcal U$ to $\int gf_*$ for every bounded
test $g$. The dual identification in Theorem~\ref{thm:L1_dual_finite}
and finite intersections of filter sets show that $\mathcal U$
converges to $f_*$ in the weak topology of $L^1$.

Since $W$ is weakly closed, this limit lies in $W$: otherwise its open
complement would give an empty trace on $W$ in a proper ultrafilter.
Every proper ultrafilter on $W$ thus converges in $W$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:compact_ultrafilter} makes $W$ weakly compact.
The zero-measure case is included, since both function spaces then
consist of their zero equivalence class.
\Done

This establishes topological weak compactness in the finite-measure
$L^1$ setting, not an unrestricted theorem that every sequentially
compact subset of every topological vector space is compact. The
ultrafilter proof uses weak compactness of Hilbert balls and uniform
truncation; the reverse implication uses the separable span of a given
sequence. These extra arguments are the bridge that a bare reference
to convergence of sequences would omit.

\paragraph{Why the hypotheses matter.}
The small-set lemma shows why both norm boundedness and control of
concentration matter. An $L^1$ bound alone does not control concentration
on small sets.
For example, on $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure, let
$f_n=n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}$. Every $f_n$ has norm one, but for
$n>M$ its tail integral above $M$ is still one. This family therefore
fails the displayed uniform-integrability condition. It also has no
weakly convergent subsequence: testing against
$\mathbf{1}_{[\delta,1]}$, for each $\delta>0$, forces a putative
limit to vanish on $(0,1]$, while the constant test function one
would give integral one. More explicitly, the same test works for
every measurable subset of $[\delta,1]$, so the conclusion is
almost-everywhere vanishing, not merely zero integral on an interval.

Nor should the finite-measure hypothesis be dropped silently. On
$\mathbb{R}$ with Lebesgue measure, the functions
$f_n=\mathbf{1}_{[n,n+1)}$ satisfy the tail condition, since they are
bounded by one. They have no weakly convergent subsequence in $L^1$:
a putative weak limit has integral zero over every bounded measurable
set and hence is zero almost everywhere, whereas testing against the
constant function one would give integral one. This example exhibits
mass escaping the finite regions of the carrier. A criterion for an
infinite measure space needs additional hypotheses or a stronger
uniform-integrability convention.

Here ``Dunford--Pettis'' denotes the equivalent weak-compactness and
sequential criteria proved for subsets of real $L^1$ on a finite measure space.
The compact-operator results earlier in the chapter concern norm-compact
images and their consequences for weakly convergent sequences. An
operator property or an integrability notion for vector-valued functions
requires its own definition and hypotheses, even when it shares the
Dunford--Pettis name.
